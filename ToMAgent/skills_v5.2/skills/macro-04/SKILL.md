# Social Cues and Nonverbal Intention

## Use When
- Use when the key evidence is a nonverbal cue such as a glance, gaze toward a target, nudge, wink, smile, nod, shrug, gesture, posture, or pause.
- Use when the question asks what the cue means, what the sender intended, or what social action (invite, warn, signal, recruit, refuse, tease) the cue performs.
- Use when the cue is directed at a specific person, object, or place and you must recover the intended message from that direction plus the shared context.
- Use when the question asks what a character is about to do and a nonverbal cue (a beckon, a head shake, a directed look) is the main basis for predicting that action.

## Do Not Use When
- Do not use when the key evidence is a spoken utterance whose hidden meaning must be decoded (indirect language, a hint, sarcasm, a polite request). That is conversational-implicature reasoning: work out the implied meaning of the words, not the meaning of a body cue.
- Do not use when the question asks only what a character FEELS, with no demand to recover what they are signaling to someone else. That is an emotion-inference scene: infer the feeling from the situation that caused it, not a communicative intent.
- Do not use when the answer turns on who knows what, who saw what, or what a character mistakenly believes (false belief, appearance-reality, knowledge boundary). Track each character's information access instead.
- Do not use when the cue is only incidental background and the real question is moral blame, apology, relationship repair, or status, which require reasoning about norms and consequences rather than cue meaning.

## Decision Variable
what intention or social action a nonverbal cue conveys

## Direct Route Rule
Route here when the central evidence is a nonverbal cue (a look, gaze direction, gesture, nudge, smile, nod, wink, shrug, posture, or timed pause) and the question asks what that cue means, what the sender intends, or what the cue signals the person will do next.

## Trigger Checklist
- The central evidence is a nonverbal cue (look, gaze direction, nudge, gesture, smile, nod, wink, shrug, posture, or pause), not a sentence whose words must be interpreted.
- The question asks what the cue means, what the sender intends, or what the cue signals the person is about to do.
- The cue is directed at a specific target or in a specific direction (toward a person, an object, or a place).
- The cue functions socially: to signal, coordinate, invite, warn, refuse, tease, reassure, or recruit help.
- The scene is solvable without first decoding the implied meaning of a spoken utterance.

## Workflow
- Step 1 - Pin the cue and its target/direction. Name the exact nonverbal act (a glance, a head tilt, a nudge, a smile, a beckon) and WHO or WHAT it is aimed at (the person it is shown to, and where the gaze or gesture points). The target and direction are the strongest clues to intent; never read the cue in isolation.
- Step 2 - List candidate intentions. Enumerate the plausible social actions the cue could perform here: invitation, a directive to act or stop, a request for agreement, a warning, a shared joke, recruiting help or an idea, or a mere emotion display with no message. Keep two or three real candidates in play.
- Step 3 - Disambiguate with immediate shared context. Use the activity the two were just engaged in, what they were just discussing, and where the gaze points to pick among the candidates. The shared context (e.g. they were stuck deciding where to go; the gaze lands on a third person) selects the single intended meaning. This step is where most answers are won or lost.
- Step 4 - Check the response. If a partner nods, stands, mouths a word, or acts after the cue, that uptake confirms which intention was understood; use it to ratify your reading.
- Step 5 - Reject distractors. Drop options that are over-literal about the gesture, that stop at a generic emotion label when a message is clearly being sent, or that add facts (a joke, a secret) the context does not support.
- Step 6 - State the single best intention. Commit to one intended social act, phrased as what the sender wants the receiver to know or do.

## Special Case
**A gaze or gesture aimed at a third party recruits a shared action**: When a character gives a partner a meaningful look and directs gaze (or a nudge or beckon) toward a specific third person or place, read the cue as proposing a joint action involving that target, then let the partner's response and the prior conversation fix exactly which action.

Scene: Rosa and Fang are watching other children play and discussing the park. Rosa gives Fang a look, glances toward the swings where a new child sits, then smiles at Fang. Fang nods, and both stand up.
Question: Why does Rosa smile at Fang?
Answer logic: Pin the cue: a directed look plus a gaze toward the new child on the swings, ending in a smile at Fang. Candidates: she is just amused, she likes after-school play, she is proposing they do something about that child. The gaze target (the new child) plus Fang's nod and both standing up select 'let's go over and make a new friend together.' Reject the generic emotion readings (amused, likes playing) because they ignore the gaze direction and the joint move that follows.

## Boundary Exit Rule
- If the load-bearing evidence is a spoken sentence whose real meaning is indirect (a hint, a polite request, sarcasm), the task is decoding conversational implicature: work out what the words imply, not what a body cue means. The cue-reading procedure here does not apply.
- If the question asks only how a character feels (and especially a bystander who cannot read a private signal), infer the emotion from the situation that caused it; do not convert it into a communicative-intent question.
- If the answer depends on what a character knows, saw, or mistakenly believes, reason about each character's information access and false beliefs instead of cue meaning.
- If the cue merely accompanies a lie and the answer turns on whether a statement is true or deceptive, evaluate the truth status of the utterance rather than the cue.
- When more than one character could have noticed the cue and that matters, reason explicitly about which character had line of sight or attention on the cue, and when.
- When the cue clearly stands in for a speech act (a request, a refusal, a warning), translate it into that speech act and answer as if it had been said aloud.
