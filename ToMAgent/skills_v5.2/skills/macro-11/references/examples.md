# macro-11 Examples and Boundaries

## Decision Variable
what the character does next when an existing commitment, plan, or in-progress task competes with a new opportunity or interruption

## Route Signal
The character already has a plan, promise, appointment, or task underway, and a new option appears that could interrupt or redirect them; the question asks what they DO next.

## Hard Boundary
- Reading what a gesture, glance, or hint means, or what the character wants or is signaling
- Settling a shared choice among two or more people's conflicting preferences
- A simple dominant desire satisfied through a deal or detour, with no prior commitment
- Choosing polite wording, persuading a listener, or judging rudeness or offense

## Shortcut To Avoid
- Do not answer what the character wants or is hinting at when the question asks what they will do next.
- Do not reflexively continue the started task: check whether relief was arranged or the task is already finished before deciding.
- Do not over-focus on the appealing new opportunity and silently drop a standing promise or in-progress task the scene implies resumes.
- Do not treat a polite invitation as a binding order, or a scheduling question as a question of general preference.

## Common Failure Modes
- Confusing what the character WANTS or is signaling with what they will actually DO next.
- Dropping the original commitment when the scene clearly implies it resumes after the interruption is handled.
- Ignoring an explicit promise, duty, or already-started task and answering with the most appealing option instead.
- Reflexively defaulting to 'continue the task' even when the scene shows someone reliable has taken it over or it is already done.

## Minimal Pair
- Case: A friend is cooking dinner when a movie invitation arrives; a sibling offers to take over the cooking, and the question is what the friend does next. | Why: A standing in-progress task competes with a new invitation and the question asks the next concrete action; because the cooking is credibly covered, the commitment is discharged and going to the movie is the resolved next step.
- Case: During an argument over a trip destination, a character glances at a friend's snow-mountain guidebook and then steers the talk toward snow-mountain photography; the question asks what that character wants. | Why: This is a near miss: it asks the purpose behind an indirect cue, not the next action under a competing commitment, so it belongs to the nonverbal-intention scene rather than here.

## Boundary Stress Test
- If the new opportunity is tempting but the old commitment is an explicit promise and no relief is arranged, this macro still applies and the answer favors the commitment.
- If the scene shows a reliable person taking over the started task, this macro still applies but the next action flips to taking the new opportunity, because the commitment is now covered.
- If the question asks what a look, hint, or topic shift means or what the character wants, leave this macro for the nonverbal-intention scene.
- If the puzzle is whose preference wins among several people with no prior plan, leave this macro for the preference-conflict scene.

## Generalization Note
This macro mirrors everyday planning behavior: people resume promises, schedules, and in-progress tasks after an interruption unless a new event is genuinely more urgent or the original task has been covered or completed.
