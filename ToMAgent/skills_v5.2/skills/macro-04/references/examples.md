# macro-04 Examples and Boundaries

## Decision Variable
what intention or social action a nonverbal cue conveys

## Route Signal
A character produces a look, gaze toward a target, nudge, smile, nod, wink, shrug, gesture, posture shift, or pause, and the question asks what that nonverbal cue means, what the sender intends, or what the person will do next.

## Hard Boundary
- Not for recovering the implied meaning of a spoken utterance (a hint, indirect request, or sarcasm) when no body cue carries the message.
- Not for questions that ask only how someone feels, especially a bystander who cannot read the private signal.
- Not for questions that turn on who knows or saw what, or on a mistaken belief.
- Not for moral judgment, apology, or trust questions where the cue is only incidental.

## Shortcut To Avoid
- Do not read the gesture over-literally (a beckon is not always 'come help me'; a pointed phone is not 'look how crowded it is'); read its social function in this context.
- Do not stop at a generic emotion label ('surprised', 'happy') when the cue is clearly aimed at someone and is doing communicative work.
- Do not ignore the gaze target or the activity the pair was just engaged in; that shared context is what picks the right intention.
- Do not apply word-meaning (implicature) reasoning to a cue whose evidence is a body signal, not a sentence.

## Common Failure Modes
- Over-literal reading of the gesture: taking a beckon, point, or look at face value instead of as a directed social act (a frequent error on 'what does X want to do?' prediction items).
- Ignoring the shared-context disambiguator: choosing an intention that fits the cue alone while contradicting what the characters were just doing or discussing.
- Defaulting to the most generic intention: collapsing the cue into a bland emotion ('amused', 'surprised') or the blandest social move, missing the specific invitation, warning, secret, or request actually being signaled.
- Answering a different question: giving the sender's signaled intention when the question instead asks how a bystander feels, or vice versa.

## Minimal Pair
- Case: Rosa gives Fang a meaningful look, glances toward a new child on the swings, and smiles; Fang nods and both stand up. Question: why does Rosa smile at Fang? | Why: In scope. The evidence is a nonverbal sequence directed at a target, and the gaze plus the partner's uptake fix the intended joint action (go meet the new child). This is reading a cue to recover an immediate social goal.
- Case: A roommate finds the bathroom dirty after the other has bathed and says, 'Can't you find the cleaner?' Question: what does she really want to say? | Why: Out of scope, near miss. There is no body cue to read; the meaning lives entirely in an indirect spoken utterance whose implicature ('clean it now') must be decoded. That is conversational-meaning reasoning over words, a different scene from reading a glance or gesture.

## Boundary Stress Test
- Same glance-and-gesture scene, but the question asks how a watching third party feels: this is emotion inference about a bystander who cannot read the private signal, not cue decoding.
- Same scene, but the answer depends on the exact wording of a spoken hint: this is decoding the implied meaning of an utterance, not a body cue.
- Same scene, but the key issue is which character had line of sight on the cue: reason about perceptual access and knowledge, not the cue's social meaning.
- Same scene, but the question is about offense, embarrassment, or whether someone should apologize: this is a social-norm and repair question, not cue interpretation.

## Generalization Note
The reusable skill is mapping a directed nonverbal cue to the single social action it performs, by combining the cue's target/direction with the immediate shared context and the partner's response. The invariant is a body signal used as a communicative act whose meaning is fixed by context, not memorization of what any particular gesture 'means'.
