# Fairness and Reciprocity

## Use When
- Use when the question is whether an existing split of resources, costs, effort, or favors is fair - not how to design a new one.
- Use when the scene asks if someone is taking advantage, freeloading, overpaying, or failing to repay a favor or pull their weight.
- Use when the answer turns on reciprocity: has one side received more than it has given back, and is that imbalance justified?

## Do Not Use When
- Do not use when the task is to reach a mutually acceptable deal through give-and-take or to propose a compromise - that is striking a deal, not judging a settled one (negotiation).
- Do not use when the question is who caused harm, who is at fault, or whether an apology is owed - that is blame and repair (responsibility/harm).
- Do not use when the question is whether a person is generally reliable based on their track record over time - that is reputation, not a verdict on one exchange (trust and reputation).
- Do not use when the question is what emotion an unfair situation makes someone feel (anger, indignation, gratitude, guilt) - the fairness fact may be obvious; the task is reading the feeling (moral emotions).

## Decision Variable
whether the give-and-take in this already-arranged exchange is balanced and reciprocal, or one-sidedly favors one party

## Trigger Checklist
- Is the arrangement already in place (a standing favor, an existing split, an outstanding debt) rather than still being negotiated?
- Is one side giving, paying, or carrying more than the other over the exchange?
- Is there an expected return - a repaid favor, a turn taken, a shared burden - that one side is or is not honoring?
- Does the question ask for a verdict ('is this fair / is X taking advantage'), not a plan ('what split should they agree on') or a feeling ('how does X feel')?

## Workflow
- Confirm the task is a fairness VERDICT on a settled arrangement. If instead they are still bargaining toward a split, stop - that is negotiation; if the question asks for an emotion, stop - that is moral emotions.
- Name the two sides and tally what each gave, received, or still owes (money, effort, favors, turns, burden).
- Compare the tallies: is the give-and-take roughly balanced, or does one side consistently benefit more than it returns?
- Test any imbalance for legitimate justification - a prior favor being repaid, an agreed role, proportional contribution, or differing capacity - before calling it unfair.
- State the verdict: fair / reciprocal, or unfair because one side takes more than it gives back. Do NOT answer with a proposed compromise, an apology, or a character rating.

## Special Case
**Implicit favor debt (no explicit deal)**: Apply this unit even when there is no contract or stated agreement: a social norm of reciprocity can create an obligation to return a favor or share a burden, and the fairness verdict rests on whether that implicit debt is being honored.

Scene: For months Sam has driven Lee to work every day. Lee never offers gas money, never takes a turn driving, and recently asked Sam for even more rides.
Question: Is Lee being fair to Sam?
Answer logic: There is no written deal, but repeated one-sided help creates a norm-based debt. Tally the exchange: Sam gives daily transport and cost; Lee gives nothing back and asks for more. The give-and-take is not reciprocal and no role or prior favor justifies it, so Lee is taking advantage - unfair. (Note: if the question instead asked 'how should Sam feel,' that would be moral emotions; if it asked 'what arrangement should they agree on,' that would be negotiation.)

## Boundary Exit Rule
- If the question is how to reach an acceptable arrangement or what compromise to offer, this is striking a deal through give-and-take, not judging a settled one - negotiation.
- If the question is who caused damage or who deserves blame, this is fault and repair, not exchange balance - responsibility for harm.
- If the question is whether someone is dependable in general from their history, this is long-term reliability, not a verdict on one exchange - trust and reputation.
- If the question asks what feeling the situation produces (anger, gratitude, guilt), this is reading the emotion a fairness judgment triggers, not the fairness judgment itself - moral emotions.
