# Negotiation, Concession, and Joint Plan

## Use When
- Use when two parties each hold a real stake in the outcome and the answer is the shape of the compromise both can accept.
- Use when the best move requires giving up or trading something on BOTH sides, not just convincing one side.
- Use when the scene asks for a split, swap, or middle plan that leaves each party with part of what they wanted.

## Do Not Use When
- Do not use when only one side wants something and the other is merely a target to be moved or convinced; that is persuasion, even if the speaker offers a sweetener.
- Do not use when the task is to judge whether a split or exchange is fair rather than to reach a deal; that is fairness evaluation.
- Do not use when one person is choosing among their own competing goals with no second party adjusting; that is goal-conflict arbitration.
- Do not use when the central problem is a power gap and the move is command, permission, or deference; that is choosing a communication move across a power gap (the power-navigation / hierarchical-communication capability).

## Decision Variable
whether BOTH parties adjust their terms (bilateral concession) to form a joint plan, or only one side is moved

## Trigger Checklist
- There are two parties whose wants conflict.
- Each party holds a stake the other must accommodate, so neither can simply be talked out of it.
- There is a tradeable or divisible dimension (time, money, tasks, turns, scope) to swap or split.
- The accepted outcome leaves BOTH sides changed: each gives something and gets something.

## Workflow
- State each party's interest and identify what each one will NOT give up versus what is flexible.
- Test directionality: does only one party want a change (the other is a target)? If yes, route to persuasion and stop.
- Find the tradeable or divisible dimension where the two interests can be swapped or split.
- Construct a concession from EACH side that meets enough of both interests (I give X, you give Y).
- Confirm the plan is jointly acceptable: each party is left better than no deal, and neither has simply submitted. Pick that option over any 'I win' or 'just convince them' option.

## Special Case
**A sweetener is not a concession**: An offer of payment, a favor, or a reward to get one's own way is still one-way persuasion, because the other party's underlying want is bought off, not accommodated. It becomes negotiation only when BOTH parties hold a stake in the outcome and BOTH change their terms to reach it.

Scene: Xiao Ming needs Xiao Qiang's computer tonight, but Xiao Qiang has plans to game with friends. Option: 'You game elsewhere tonight, and you get my computer all day tomorrow.'
Question: Is this negotiation or persuasion?
Answer logic: Only Xiao Ming wants a change; the original frame is one-sided ('how does Xiao Ming get the computer'). Offering milk tea or a reward is a sweetener that buys Xiao Qiang off, so it stays persuasion. It would be negotiation only if both held a genuine stake and both shifted terms to land a shared plan.

## Boundary Exit Rule
- Exit to persuasion if only one side wants the change and the other is being moved, paid, or convinced; offering a reward does not make it bilateral.
- Exit to fairness/reciprocity if the task is to judge whether a distribution or exchange is fair, not to design a deal.
- Exit to goal-conflict arbitration if a single person is weighing their own competing goals with no second party adjusting.
- Exit to choosing a communication move across a power gap (the power-navigation / hierarchical-communication capability) if the live issue is a status gap and the move is command, permission, escalation, or deference.
