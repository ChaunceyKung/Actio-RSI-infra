# Multiple-Explanation Competition

## Use When
- Use when the task asks you to compare more than one plausible explanation for the same evidence.
- Use when the answer depends on which interpretation best accounts for the observed facts, not on judging good or bad.
- Use when the prompt asks why a behavior occurred and two or more readings of that behavior stay live because the character's own reason is not stated.

## Do Not Use When
- Do not use this lens to name the question's target variable; that is the routing step.
- Do not use it to separate direct text from inference; that is the parallel evidence-chain review lens, which runs alongside it.
- Do not use it to audit for perspective, outcome, or moral bias; that is the parallel bias-check review lens.
- Do not force a contest when only one interpretation is genuinely open; if the scene fixes the character's reason, there is nothing to compare.

## Decision Variable
which explanation best accounts for the available evidence

## Trigger Checklist
- Are there two or more plausible explanations?
- Is the task asking which interpretation is best, most likely, or most supported?
- Does the question require holding multiple readings in mind before choosing one?
- Is the choice based on explanatory fit rather than moral judgment, confidence style, or direct observation?

## Workflow
- List the plausible explanations that remain open.
- Check each explanation against every available clue.
- Prefer the explanation that explains more clues with fewer assumptions.
- Reject explanations that require unsupported leaps or ignore key evidence.
- Return the best-fitting explanation, not the one that feels most familiar.

## Special Case
**Same evidence, different stories**: Use this skill when the scene provides one set of clues that could support multiple stories, and the task is to choose the strongest story rather than to judge the people involved. The scene must not state the character's own reason, so the explanations genuinely compete.

Scene: Jianning and Mingkai are co-captains and still need one more team member; they joke that the unselected players are 'the best players'. Mingkai goes quiet, winks at Jianning, looks at Taotao, one of the remaining players, then turns his head and smiles at Jianning. Jianning nods and prepares to choose Taotao.
Question: Why does Mingkai turn his head to look at Jianning and smile?
Answer logic: Several readings are live: an old joke, thinking Taotao is best, a secret plan, or a shared understanding that Taotao is the only pick left. Test each against all the clues. Only one member is missing, Taotao is the sole remaining choice, and the wink plus smile signals mutual understanding, so the reading that they both know they can only choose Taotao fits the most clues with the fewest extra assumptions. Choose that, not the most vivid or the first obvious story.

## Boundary Exit Rule
- Sorting what evidence exists or where it came from is the parallel evidence-chain lens's job, not this one's.
- Checking for bias, moral evaluation, or blame is the parallel bias-check lens's job.
- Naming a mental-state fact such as a belief, desire, or intention is the routing step's and the solver's job.
- This lens is complete once the best-supported reading among the open interpretations is chosen.
