# macro-18 Examples and Boundaries

## Decision Variable
how conflicting sides can reach a mutually acceptable plan through concessions

## Route Signal
A conflict scene asks for a workable agreement: both sides have interests, both sides can adjust, and the answer should be a concession-based or trade-off-based joint plan.

## Hard Boundary
- Not one-way persuasion: if only the listener must be convinced and the speaker's goal stays fixed and the speaker concedes nothing, it is persuasion; solve by finding the appeal that moves the listener.
- Not a preference-conflict decision: if the task is only to pick the single best allocation or outcome among conflicting wants, with no exchange of concessions, judge the outcome on the merits instead of modeling a bargain.
- Not pure misunderstanding: if clarifying the facts alone dissolves the conflict, resolve what each party meant rather than trading terms.
- Not pure apology: if the main answer is remorse and repair after an offense, lead with acknowledgment, not bargaining.
- Not pure power communication: if rank decides permission, escalation, or deference more than mutual trade-offs, reason about the hierarchy first.

## Shortcut To Avoid
- Do not pick the loudest, highest-status, or first-mentioned party as the winner.
- Do not split the difference automatically when one side has a fixed constraint and the other has a flexible preference.
- Do not confuse a compromise with both sides being equally unhappy; a good compromise protects each side's most important interest.
- Do not propose vague cooperation without a concrete allocation, concession, or next step.

## Common Failure Modes
- Producing a one-sided win: moving only the other party while the proposer concedes nothing. That is persuasion, not negotiation; a real joint plan requires BOTH sides to give something up.
- Just picking an outcome: choosing whichever single want should prevail without constructing any exchange or trade between the parties.
- Ignoring one party's must-have: trampling a non-negotiable need, so the proposed plan is one the slighted side would actually reject.
- Ignoring hidden interests behind stated positions, so the trade space that would have satisfied both is never found.
- Assuming fairness means equal division even when needs, costs, deadlines, or obligations differ between the sides.
- Offering vague cooperation or an emotionally appealing gesture that is not a specific, implementable allocation or concession.
- Forgetting the relationship or trust cost of an aggressive bargain that wins terms but damages future cooperation.

## Minimal Pair
- Case: Two coworkers both want the same Friday off; one has an unmovable family event, the other just wants a long weekend and is flexible about which Friday. They settle on the first taking this Friday and the second taking a different Friday plus a shift swap. | Why: Both sides hold conflicting preferences and BOTH adjust: one keeps the fixed date, the other moves dates and covers a swap, yielding a plan each accepts.
- Case: A child wants to go to the amusement park; the parent objects that it is too noisy. The child argues 'we could go early when it is quieter' to win the parent over, while still aiming squarely at the amusement park. | Why: This does NOT match: the child's goal never moves and the child concedes nothing real; the task is to find the appeal that shifts the parent, which is one-way persuasion.
- Case: Given one outdoorsy friend and one indoor-loving friend, the question asks only which single venue best suits an outing the indoor friend is planning, and the answer is the one option both could enjoy. | Why: This does NOT match: it asks only which outcome to pick among conflicting tastes, with no back-and-forth concessions; it is a preference-conflict decision judged by the result, not a bargaining process.

## Boundary Stress Test
- If one side tries to convince the other to accept a plan, ask whether the persuader ALSO changes their own terms. If the persuader's goal is fixed and they concede nothing, it is one-way persuasion; only treat it as negotiation when both sides give something up.
- If the prompt simply asks which single option, venue, or allocation conflicting parties should pick, ask whether any concessions are exchanged. If the answer is just the best outcome on the merits, it is a preference-conflict decision, not a bargained joint plan.
- If one side apologizes and offers to pay for damage, ask whether the core answer is the act of repair or the specific remedy terms; treat it as negotiation only when settling the terms is the main issue.
- If a supervisor and employee bargain over deadlines, keep this scope only if mutual trade-offs are central; if rank governs the appropriate move, reason about the hierarchy first.
- If the parties clash because they interpreted a statement differently, first check whether clarifying the facts alone removes the conflict before constructing any trade.

## Generalization Note
This macro generalizes across family decisions, workplace scheduling, roommate conflicts, resource allocation, team planning, service disputes, and diplomatic-style trade-offs whenever the solution requires reciprocal adjustment around underlying interests.
