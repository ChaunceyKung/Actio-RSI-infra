# Power Navigation and Hierarchical Communication

## Use When
- Use when the question asks which communication MOVE someone should make across a power, rank, or authority gap.
- Use when a person must choose how to speak up or down a hierarchy: comply, ask permission, defer, push back, escalate, delegate, challenge, or retreat.
- Use when the deciding factor is relative power — the right move would change if the other party's authority changed.

## Do Not Use When
- Do not use when the question only asks who has what role, title, rank, or authority; naming the structure belongs with identifying the power/role structure, not choosing a move.
- Do not use when the task is one-way persuasion that changes a belief or attitude without a power gap deciding the move; that is general persuasion.
- Do not use when the only issue is wording the same message for the listener's comprehension level; that is audience calibration.
- Do not use when the key problem is compromise or bilateral trade-off between equals; that is negotiation.
- Do not use when the main issue is self-presentation or image rather than hierarchy-sensitive action.

## Decision Variable
the communication move that best fits the power gap

## Trigger Checklist
- Is there a clear hierarchy, rank, authority difference, or gatekeeper?
- Does the question ask what the actor should DO (a move), not merely who is in charge (a label)?
- Are the candidate answers communication moves — comply, ask permission, defer, push back, escalate, delegate, challenge, or withdraw?
- Would the best move change if the other party's power level changed, i.e., is the gap load-bearing rather than truth, emotion, or shared preference?

## Workflow
- Confirm the question asks WHICH MOVE the actor should make (comply, defer, ask permission, push back, escalate, delegate, retreat), not merely WHO outranks whom. If it only asks for the role or rank, this is the wrong unit (see Boundary Exit Rule).
- Map the power direction: is the actor speaking UP to someone with more authority, DOWN to a subordinate, or to a gatekeeper who controls access? The direction sets which moves are even available.
- State the actor's goal: get a request granted, resist or push back on a decision, report a problem, win approval, or assign work.
- Pick the move that achieves the goal at the LOWEST relational cost given the gap. Speaking up: prefer evidence, a respectful concrete proposal, or a request for a follow-up over blunt 'you are wrong' or going behind the person's back. Speaking down: a plain direct request often beats wielding rank or dangling rewards.
- Eliminate options that ignore the power gap (treating a superior like a peer, leaning on loyalty/effort instead of substance) and options that overshoot it (public defiance, threats, or skipping the person entirely when a normal channel exists).
- If the direct move is genuinely blocked by the hierarchy, choose the strategic fallback: escalate through the proper channel, seek mediation, or retreat — not a louder version of the blocked move.

## Special Case
**Pushing back UP: substance over loyalty, and never behind the person's back**: When a subordinate disagrees with a superior's judgment, the best move advances the case with objective substance the superior can evaluate, addressed to the superior directly. Reject moves that lean on the actor's effort/loyalty (the gap means that is not persuasive) and reject moves that route around the superior through other people (that breaks the channel and signals disrespect for their authority).

Scene: An engineer proposes a new software idea in a work report. His leader thinks the plan has technical and economic feasibility problems.
Question: What move should the engineer make in response to the leader's objection?
Answer logic: Best move: present successful cases of similar plans at other companies — objective evidence the leader can weigh on the merits. Wrong: pleading that he works hard so the leader should trust him (effort does not answer a feasibility objection across the gap); and persuading the leader through other colleagues or back channels (going around the authority figure undermines the proper channel). The deciding factor is the move's fit to the power relation, not whether the engineer's idea is actually good.

## Boundary Exit Rule
- Exit if the question only asks who outranks whom or what someone's role is — that names the structure; route to identifying the power/role structure.
- Exit if it is one-way persuasion to change a belief with no power gap deciding the move; route to general persuasion.
- Exit if the task is only adjusting wording for the listener's level; route to audience calibration.
- Exit if the scene is compromise or bargaining between equals; route to negotiation.
- Exit if the main judgment is image, politeness style, or emotional display rather than a hierarchy-driven move.
