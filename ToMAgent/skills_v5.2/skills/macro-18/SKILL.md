# Negotiation, Compromise, and Joint Plan

## Use When
- Use when two or more sides have partly conflicting goals and the answer depends on finding a mutually acceptable plan.
- Use when both sides must adjust, concede, trade, prioritize, or coordinate rather than one side simply winning.
- Use when the question asks what offer, compromise, joint plan, or next negotiation move would work best.
- Use when preferences, obligations, fairness, relationship preservation, or long-term cooperation shape the agreement.

## Do Not Use When
- Do not use for one-way persuasion where one speaker keeps a fixed goal and only the listener must change; there, the task is to find the appeal or incentive that moves the listener, with no concession from the speaker.
- Do not use when the question only asks which single allocation, choice, or outcome is best among conflicting wants, with no back-and-forth exchange; that is a preference-conflict decision judged by the result, not a bargaining process.
- Do not use for live misunderstanding repair unless, after the facts are clarified, a substantive plan still has to be bargained out.
- Do not use for apology or reconciliation unless the answer turns on the concrete negotiated remedy or compensation rather than the act of making amends.
- Do not use for self-presentation or impression management unless the core task is actually reaching an agreement.
- Do not use for hierarchy-only communication unless mutual trade-offs, not rank, decide the right move.

## Decision Variable
how conflicting sides can reach a mutually acceptable plan through concessions

## Direct Route Rule
Route immediately when the case requires offers, concessions, trade-offs, or a shared solution under conflict.

## Expand With Micro Units
- Self Preference: the character's own preference (micro-19)
- Other Preference: the character's representation of another person's preference (micro-20)
- Goal-Conflict Arbitration: the weighting rule for competing goals: which active goal the character gives greater weight, and on what basis (micro-25)
- Fairness and Reciprocity: whether the give-and-take in this already-arranged exchange is balanced and reciprocal, or one-sidedly favors one party (micro-44)
- Negotiation, Concession, and Joint Plan: whether BOTH parties adjust their terms (bilateral concession) to form a joint plan, or only one side is moved (micro-51)

## Trigger Checklist
- At least two parties want different outcomes or have competing priorities.
- A successful answer requires mutual adjustment, not unilateral compliance.
- There is room for an offer, concession, trade-off, split, rotation, compensation, timing change, or shared plan.
- The scene asks what they should agree to, what would be fair, what offer would work, or how to resolve the conflict.
- Each side has interests that can be separated from its initial stated demand.
- Maintaining cooperation, fairness, trust, or future interaction matters.

## Workflow
- Name each party and state their surface position: the concrete thing each side says it wants.
- Infer each side's underlying interests: the needs, constraints, deadlines, values, or relationships driving the position. For each side, identify its one must-have (non-negotiable) versus what it can flex on or trade away.
- Separate fixed constraints from flexible preferences on BOTH sides; do not assume all demands carry equal weight or that one side's want outranks the other's by default.
- Map the trade space: look for items one side values highly that cost the other side little, plus timing swaps, taking turns, partial sharing, rotation, compensation, bundling, or conditional 'if you do X, I'll do Y' deals.
- Construct a plan in which BOTH sides give something up AND both protect their must-have. If your draft only moves one side while the other concedes nothing, it is persuasion, not a negotiated plan; revise until each side both gives and gains.
- Verify acceptability for each side in turn: would Side A accept this (its must-have is safe, its loss is bearable)? Would Side B? If either would walk away, adjust the package.
- Check feasibility and fairness: the plan must be specific and implementable, and must not secretly load the whole burden onto one party.
- If trust or future cooperation matters, add a concrete follow-up, accountability step, or respectful explanation.
- Answer with the recommended agreement, naming what each side concedes and what each side keeps, and why both would say yes.

## Special Case
**Position conflict with hidden compatible interests**: When the stated demands appear incompatible, test whether the underlying interests can be satisfied by a different package instead of splitting the visible object or choosing one winner.

Scene: Two roommates both want the only quiet room on Saturday. One needs it from 9 to 12 for an online exam; the other wants it all day to prepare a presentation due Monday.
Question: What compromise should they make?
Answer logic: Do not treat the conflict as an all-day winner-takes-room dispute. The exam has a fixed time constraint, while the presentation preparation is more flexible. A good plan gives the exam-taker the room from 9 to 12, gives the presenter the room afterward, and may add quiet rules or a backup workspace. Both sides adjust while protecting their highest-priority need.

## Boundary Exit Rule
- If only ONE side needs to move and the speaker keeps their goal fixed and offers no concession of their own, this is one-way persuasion, not negotiation. Solve it by finding the appeal, reason, or incentive that shifts the listener; do not invent a give-and-take the scene does not contain.
- If the conflict is really just a misunderstanding and simply clarifying the facts dissolves it with no trade-off needed, treat it as misunderstanding repair: establish what each party actually meant first, and check whether agreement follows automatically once the facts line up.
- If the main task is to express remorse, acknowledge harm, or make amends after an offense, treat it as apology and reconciliation: lead with acknowledgment and repair, and only bargain over terms if the scene's central question is the concrete remedy.
- If the scene only asks which single allocation, choice, or outcome is correct among conflicting wants, and there is no back-and-forth exchange of concessions, treat it as a preference-conflict decision: pick the best outcome on the merits without modeling a bargaining process.
- If the question is how someone should present themselves, introduce themselves, or manage formality or impression, that is self-presentation, not reaching agreement; reason about the impression to convey rather than terms to trade.
- If the deciding factor is power or rank (who may grant permission, escalate, or defer) rather than mutual trade-offs, reason first about the hierarchy and the appropriate move within it, treating any bargaining as secondary.
