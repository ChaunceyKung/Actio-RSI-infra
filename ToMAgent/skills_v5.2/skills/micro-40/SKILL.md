# Audience Calibration

## Use When
- Use when the task is to judge or choose wording that fits a particular listener's expertise, age, group membership, or prior stance.
- Use when the decisive variable is whether the speaker adjusted explanation depth, vocabulary, examples, or framing for that audience.
- Use when two phrasings carry the SAME goal and the question is only which one the listener can best understand or accept.
- Use when the issue is listener-fit, not influence, truth, or politeness.

## Do Not Use When
- Do not use when the goal is to change the listener's belief, attitude, or behavior; that is the persuasion-strategy capability (micro-41).
- Do not use when the question is whether the statement is misleading, true, false, or selectively framed; that is the truth-status capability (micro-37) or the misleading-expression capability (micro-38).
- Do not use when the question is WHY the speaker chose that wording (politeness, face-saving, testing); that is the speaker-motivation capability (micro-39).
- Do not use when the issue is tact, embarrassment, faux pas, or offense; that is the faux-pas-and-offense capability (micro-42).

## Decision Variable
how well the expression fits the listener's knowledge, age, identity, or stance

## Trigger Checklist
- Who is the target listener — expert, novice, child, insider, outsider, ally, or opponent?
- Does the wording match that listener's likely background knowledge and vocabulary?
- Do the candidate options share the same goal and differ only in how understandable or acceptable they are to this listener?
- Would a different audience need a different level of detail, example, or tone for the SAME message?

## Workflow
- Identify the target listener and what they likely know, value, or can follow.
- Confirm the question is about listener-fit, not about changing the listener's mind, the truth of the claim, or the speaker's motive.
- Compare the options on a single axis: which one is pitched at the right level of detail, vocabulary, and framing for this listener.
- Reject options that add jargon a novice cannot follow, talk down to or dismiss the listener, or switch the task to persuasion.
- Pick the option whose form best matches the audience profile; if the real task is to change the listener, exit to the persuasion-strategy capability.

## Special Case
**Same content, two audiences**: When the same information is delivered to two listeners, the better-calibrated version is the one matched to the named listener's level — lower jargon for a novice, more technical depth for an expert. Do not average across listeners; calibrate to the one the question targets.

Scene: A doctor explains the same treatment twice: to a medical intern using clinical terms (BID dosing, adverse-event monitoring), and to the patient's parent who has no medical training, using everyday words ('a medicine taken twice a day, and here are the side effects in plain language').
Question: Which explanation is better calibrated to the patient's parent?
Answer logic: The decision variable is listener-fit. For the lay parent the plain-language version is better calibrated because it removes jargon and matches their background knowledge; the clinical version fits the intern, not the parent.

## Boundary Exit Rule
- Exit to the persuasion-strategy capability — how to change the listener's belief, attitude, or behavior (micro-41) — if the core goal is to move the listener, not just to fit their level.
- Exit to the misleading-expression capability — whether a true statement induces a false belief (micro-38) — if the issue is hidden implication, not audience fit.
- Exit to the speaker-motivation capability — why the speaker chose that wording (micro-39) — if the question asks for the speaker's social motive.
- Exit to the faux-pas-and-offense capability (micro-42) — tact, offense, and faux pas — if the central issue is embarrassment or rudeness rather than comprehension fit.
