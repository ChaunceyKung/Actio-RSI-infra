# micro-51 Examples and Boundaries

## Decision Variable
whether BOTH parties adjust their terms (bilateral concession) to form a joint plan, or only one side is moved

## Route Signal
Route here when a scene asks how two parties, each with a real stake, swap or split a tradeable dimension so both end up with part of what they wanted.

## Hard Boundary
- Bilateral adjustment is mandatory: both parties must change terms. One-way influence (even with a reward attached) is persuasion, not negotiation.
- The target is the structure of the deal (who gives and gets what), not a verdict on whether an existing split is fair (that is fairness and reciprocity).
- Two distinct parties are required; a single agent balancing their own goals is goal-conflict arbitration, not negotiation.

## Shortcut To Avoid
- Do not treat every disagreement as negotiation; most persuasion stories have only one party who wants the change.
- Do not count an offer of payment, milk tea, or a favor as a concession; a sweetener buys the other off and stays one-way.
- Do not pick the option that fully wins for one side; negotiation needs a mutually acceptable middle.
- Do not answer from a fairness verdict; the diagnostic is whether both sides change terms, not whether a split looks fair.

## Common Failure Modes
- Labeling a one-way persuasion scene as negotiation because the speaker offers a reward or alternative.
- Choosing the 'I get exactly what I want' option instead of the mutually acceptable plan.
- Missing the tradeable dimension (time, turns, scope) that lets both sides give and get.
- Confusing a fairness judgment about a split with the act of negotiating that split.

## Minimal Pair
- Case: Xiao Hong wants the amusement park; Dad objects to the noise; she suggests going to a park or zoo instead so everyone is happy. | Why: NOT negotiation -> persuasion. The frame is one-way ('how does Xiao Hong persuade her dad'); offering an alternative is a concession-flavored persuasion move, not two parties each shifting terms over a shared stake.
- Case: Two teammates both want the single conference slot; one agrees to present later if the other prepares the slides and handles setup. | Why: IS negotiation. Both hold a real stake in the slot, and both change terms over a tradeable dimension (timing for labor), leaving each with part of what they wanted.

## Boundary Stress Test
- If one side gives in and the other does not move at all, it is persuasion or compliance, not negotiation.
- If a reward or favor is offered to get one's own way, the frame is still one-sided -> persuasion.
- If the question is 'was this split fair?' rather than 'what deal do they strike?', route to fairness.
- If a lone person is weighing their own competing goals, route to goal-conflict arbitration.

## Generalization Note
Generalizes to any two-party exchange where the diagnostic is reciprocal concession toward a shared plan, across friendships, work, family, and resource conflicts. The decisive test is always directionality: two genuine stakes plus mutual adjustment, versus one party being moved.
