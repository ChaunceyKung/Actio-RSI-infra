# macro-09 Examples and Boundaries

## Decision Variable
which message moves this specific listener toward the persuader's fixed goal by answering the listener's stated concern while staying true to the persuader's real motive

## Route Signal
The scene asks how to phrase, frame, explain, advise, encourage, warn, or persuade a particular listener so the listener will change belief, attitude, or behavior.

## Hard Boundary
- Not for negotiation, compromise, or joint decision making.
- Not for pure literal comprehension or indirect speech alone.
- Not for truth evaluation alone.
- Not for cases where the listener is irrelevant to the answer.

## Shortcut To Avoid
- Do not assume the most detailed, technical, or rational option is automatically the most persuasive; it loses when the listener's resistance is emotional or relational.
- Do not pick a compromise that drops the persuader's original goal (offering a different activity, place, or plan) when the task is to win the listener over to that goal.
- Do not ignore the one specific objection the listener actually voiced and answer with a generic appeal to a generic audience.
- Do not reward dishonest, manipulative, coercive, or pressure-by-authority answers; honest, listener-respecting appeals are the ones that actually persuade without straining the relationship.

## Common Failure Modes
- Picking the over-rational, over-engineered answer (citing studies, data, or a technical fix) when the listener's concern is emotional, relational, or about a value, so a sincere personal appeal was the better fit.
- Picking a compromise or alternative that abandons the persuader's goal, when the aim is to move the listener toward the original goal, not to switch to a different one (that crosses into negotiation).
- Ignoring the listener's specific stated objection and choosing a generic appeal, instead of the option that directly answers that exact worry.
- Choosing a feelings-based 'just trust me' appeal when the listener's objection was actually about feasibility or evidence and required concrete proof or a demonstration.
- Confusing this one-way influence scene with two-way bargaining or with a scene about whose differing preference gets accommodated, and answering for the wrong scene type.

## Minimal Pair
- Case: A child wants the family to go to the amusement park; the father objects that it is too noisy, and the child sincerely says she has not been in a long time and it really means a lot to her. | Why: One-way influence with the persuader's goal held fixed: the answer addresses the listener's relationship with the persuader and her honest motive, rather than over-engineering a fix for the literal noise objection or compromising to a different place.
- Case: An outdoor-loving man invites a quiet librarian for the weekend and they want different things, so he proposes the library. | Why: This is NOT one-way persuasion. Neither person is being talked out of an objection; the actor accommodates the OTHER person's discrepant preference and changes his own plan, so the task is to predict whose desire wins, not to craft a persuasive message.

## Boundary Stress Test
- If the listener voiced one concrete objection, check that the chosen answer speaks to THAT objection, not to a different concern you imagined for them.
- If a compromise option appears (do a different activity, go elsewhere, split the difference), ask whether the task is really to win the listener to the original goal; if so, the compromise that drops the goal is usually the wrong answer, not the right one.
- If both sides can change their offer and the answer is the deal they reach, it is two-way bargaining, not persuasion.
- If the two people merely want different things and the answer is which preference is accommodated, it is a whose-desire-wins judgment, not message crafting.
- If the listener is a skeptical expert, a doubtful buyer, or someone who thinks the persuader is lying, prefer concrete proof or a demonstration over an emotional appeal; if the listener is a close relation resisting on feelings or values, prefer the sincere personal appeal over technical detail.

## Generalization Note
In general, the persuasive answer is the one that (a) keeps the persuader's real goal fixed, (b) directly addresses the listener's single specific stated concern, (c) authentically reflects the persuader's true motive, and (d) fits the relationship, choosing an emotional or relational appeal for feelings-based resistance and concrete proof for evidence-based resistance. Answers that over-rationalize, abandon the goal via compromise, ignore the stated objection, or rely on manipulation consistently lose. This covers advice, encouragement, warning, and explanation as well as direct persuasion.
