# macro-08 Examples and Boundaries

## Decision Variable
how an utterance operates across truth value, motive, and misleading effect

## Route Signal
The scene asks whether a speaker told the truth or lied, told a half-truth, gave a cover story, or used wording to create a false impression - and may ask the motive (protect feelings, avoid blame) or whether a false statement was even intentional.

## Hard Boundary
- A non-literal line whose point is its intended meaning (irony, sarcasm, joke) rather than its truth value is not this scene, even when the words sound false.
- An honest indirect message (hint, request, warning, refusal) that creates no false belief is not this scene.
- Persuasion or audience-tailoring of a sincerely believed message, with no truth-status problem, is not this scene.
- A moral-judgment or blame scene (was the lie wrong, who is at fault) is not this scene unless the task is to classify the utterance itself.

## Shortcut To Avoid
- Do not call every literally-false statement a lie: if the speaker sincerely but mistakenly believes it, it is an involuntary error, not deception.
- Do not treat a true-sounding line as honest: a statement can be factually correct on a narrow point yet framed to mislead (a half-truth).
- Do not read a sarcastic or joking false line as a lie; the speaker means the opposite and is not trying to deceive.
- Do not answer from surface wording alone without checking the actual facts and what the speaker knew and intended.

## Common Failure Modes
- Calling a sincere-but-mistaken statement a lie because the words turned out to be false, ignoring that the speaker had no intent to deceive.
- Reading an ironic or sarcastic false-sounding line as a lie instead of as mockery or humor whose real meaning is the opposite of the words.
- Missing a half-truth because the literal words are factually correct, and so judging a misleading statement as honest.
- Naming the wrong motive: confusing a white lie told to spare someone's feelings with a self-serving lie told to avoid blame or gain advantage.
- Focusing only on the listener's misunderstanding while ignoring whether the speaker intended it.

## Minimal Pair
- Case: Asked why she said she likes the new boss when she does not, the answer is that she wants to keep the office harmonious and avoid conflict (a white lie to protect feelings); contrast with a child who blames the dog for a vase she broke herself to escape blame (a self-serving lie). | Why: Both are knowing falsehoods, so both are in scope; the macro's motive-reading step is exactly what separates a feelings-protecting white lie from a blame-avoiding self-serving lie.
- Case: A girl says everyone in her class was at school, though one quiet classmate was absent and sick, because she never noticed him - she sincerely believes her statement. | Why: Boundary case: the words are literally false but there is no intent to deceive, so the correct read is a sincere mistake (involuntary), not a lie - the trap is to label any false statement a lie.
- Case: After getting soaked when the picnic is rained out, a person says 'lovely weather for a picnic'; the question is why she said it, and she means it to mock her friend's choice, not to deceive anyone. | Why: Near miss: the line is literally false, which tempts a lie reading, but its function is irony/mockery (intended meaning), so it belongs to decoding non-literal meaning, not to truth-status or deception.

## Boundary Stress Test
- If the utterance is literally true but framed to push the listener to a wrong conclusion, stay here and classify it as a misleading half-truth.
- If the utterance is literally false but the speaker sincerely believed it, leave the lie classification and call it an honest mistake.
- If the utterance is non-literal and the point is its opposite/intended meaning (sarcasm, joke, hint), leave this scene and decode the meaning instead.
- If the question is whether the lie was morally wrong or how to repair the relationship, that is a separate blame/repair problem, not utterance classification.

## Generalization Note
This macro classifies an utterance by three variables together - truth value (true / false / true-but-misleading), the speaker's motive when it is false (white lie to protect feelings, self-serving lie to avoid blame or gain advantage, or a sincere mistake that is no lie at all), and the misleading effect on the listener - covering direct lies, strategic omissions, half-truths, cover stories, and bluffs.
