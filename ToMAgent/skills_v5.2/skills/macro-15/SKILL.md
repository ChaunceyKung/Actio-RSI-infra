# Trust, Relationship, and Long-Term Interaction

## Use When
- Use when how much to trust someone, or what they will likely do next, turns on their accumulated history: how reliable they have been, whether they keep promises, whether reciprocity has been honored over repeated dealings.
- Use when the scene gives a track record over time (years of dependability, a habit of paying back, a known reputation) and asks you to read the current situation in light of it.
- Use when a single recent event must be weighed against a long pattern (one late payment after years on time; one missed deadline after years of reliability) and you must decide whether the deviation overrides the record.
- Use when reputation or a long relationship label is the reason to extend or withhold trust, lend again, delegate something sensitive, or predict whether the person will follow through.

## Do Not Use When
- Do not use when there is no track record at all and the task is to judge a single statement's reliability on its own (one message, one claim, one source) — that is a one-off credibility call, decided from the content and motive of that one statement, not from a history.
- Do not use when the core task is to allocate a resource fairly or settle who owes whom this one time — a fairness/reciprocity split is decided by a fairness rule (equal share, need, desert), not by reading a relationship's trust record.
- Do not use when harm has already been done and the scene is about apology, compensation, or rebuilding the relationship — a repair case is solved by what restores trust going forward, not by scoring the past record.
- Do not use when the real difficulty is a live misunderstanding to be cleared up in the moment, a false-belief about an object's location, or decoding what an indirect remark means — those hinge on the immediate exchange, not on accumulated history.

## Decision Variable
whether the current trust judgment and behavior prediction should be driven by the long-term track record (past reliability, repeated reciprocity, reputation) rather than by any single recent event

## Direct Route Rule
Route here when the scene supplies a relationship history over time and asks how much to trust the person now or what they will likely do, so the answer must integrate the track record rather than react to one event.

## Trigger Checklist
- The scene reports repeated past behavior or a known track record (has done X reliably for years, always pays back, keeps showing up).
- The question is how much to trust the person now, whether to rely on or delegate to them, or what they will probably do next.
- A reputation or longstanding relationship label is offered as the reason to extend or withhold trust.
- A single recent event is set against a long pattern, and you must decide whether it changes the standing judgment.
- The decisive evidence is the accumulated record over time, not the wording of one current utterance.

## Workflow
- Gather the relationship history: count how many past interactions there are, how reliable the person has been (promises kept vs broken), whether reciprocity was honored, and any reputation signals from third parties. If almost no history exists, stop — this is a one-off credibility call, not a track-record case.
- Separate the long-standing record from the single most recent event. State the established baseline first (e.g., 'reliable across many years'), then name the new event on its own.
- Weight the evidence by depth and consistency, not recency: a deep, consistent record outweighs one isolated deviation. Only let the recent event dominate if it is decisive on its own — a verified betrayal, a deliberate repeated breach, or a formal change of role — not merely the latest data point.
- Form the current trust judgment from the integrated picture and predict the likely behavior it implies (will keep following through / can be entrusted with this / is worth lending to again).
- Note explicitly when a single deviation should NOT override the record (an excusable one-off lapse against years of reliability) versus when it SHOULD (a clear, intentional, or repeated breach that breaks the pattern).

## Special Case
**One new event against a long record**: When a single new event conflicts with a long, consistent record, keep the long-term pattern as the baseline and treat the event as an exception — UNLESS the event is decisive on its own (a verified betrayal, a deliberate or repeated breach, or a formal role change), in which case the record no longer protects the judgment.

Scene: A coworker has been dependable for years but misses one deadline, right after a sudden family emergency.
Question: Should their reliability be judged as bad now?
Answer logic: Set the baseline from the multi-year record of dependability, then place the single miss beside it. The miss is one isolated, well-explained event, not a pattern or a deliberate breach, so it does not override years of reliability. Judge them as still reliable, treating this as an excusable exception, and predict they will keep following through.

## Boundary Exit Rule
- If there is no history and the task is to judge whether one statement or one source is believable on its own, solve it as a single-statement credibility call: weigh the content, the speaker's motive, and any corroboration for that one claim — do not invent a track record the scene never gave.
- If the task is to divide a resource fairly or settle a one-time who-owes-whom, solve it with a fairness rule (equal split, share by need, share by contribution) — reading the relationship's trust record is the wrong tool there.
- If harm has already happened and the scene is about apologizing, compensating, or rebuilding the bond, solve it as a repair problem — choose what restores trust going forward, rather than scoring the prior record.
- If the real difficulty is clearing up a live misunderstanding in the moment, solve it by surfacing and correcting the wrong assumption in the immediate exchange, not by appealing to history.
- If the answer really turns on a false belief about where something is, on decoding an indirect or figurative remark, or on adjusting tone for a power gap, handle it as that kind of problem; only stay here when accumulated trust history is the actual driver.
