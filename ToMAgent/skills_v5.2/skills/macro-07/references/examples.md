# macro-07 Examples and Boundaries

## Decision Variable
what the speaker really wants the listener to understand from an utterance whose literal wording differs from its intended meaning

## Route Signal
A speaker says something whose literal wording clearly does not match the situation, and the listener is expected to infer the real intended meaning: an indirect request or hint, an ironic or sarcastic reversal, or a joke.

## Hard Boundary
- Not about whether the utterance is factually true or whether the speaker intends to deceive.
- Not about estimating a quantity or likelihood from vague quantifiers, base rates, or partial samples.
- Not about meaning carried by gestures, glances, or facial expressions instead of words.
- Not about the speaker's hidden feeling for its own sake, or about how best to persuade or adapt to a listener.

## Shortcut To Avoid
- Do not assume every non-literal sentence belongs here; a statement said to deceive is a lie problem and a vague quantifier asking 'how many' is an estimation problem.
- Do not answer from literal semantics alone when context clearly signals an intended indirect meaning, irony, or joke.
- Do not invent an indirect meaning when the words plainly fit the situation and mean exactly what they say.

## Common Failure Modes
- Answering the literal sentence instead of the intended meaning (treating an indirect request as a factual comment, or taking ironic praise at face value).
- Reading irony as sincere because the words are positive, missing that the situation contradicts them.
- Treating a deceptive statement as an indirect meaning when the real issue is its truth value and intent to deceive.
- Treating a joke as a literal claim or a genuine mistake instead of a non-serious incongruity meant to amuse.
- Over-applying indirect inference to a plain literal statement that has no mismatch with context.

## Minimal Pair
- Case: Marissa finds the bathroom filthy right after Anna used it and says, 'Anna, can't you find the cleaning supplies?' | Why: In scope: the literal form is a question about ability, but in context it is an indirect request the speaker wants understood as 'clean the bathroom now'. The task is decoding the intended speech act.
- Case: Aunt Jian asks if Jianjun likes her new hat; he privately finds it ugly but says, 'Oh, it looks good.' | Why: Near miss: the speaker says something he believes is false to spare feelings, so the central issue is the truth value of the statement and the intent to deceive (a white lie), not decoding an honest-but-indirect meaning. This is a truth-value-and-intent problem, a different scene.

## Boundary Stress Test
- If an utterance is non-literal but the question asks whether it is true or why the speaker would say something false, treat it as a lie or misdirection problem, not indirect meaning.
- If an utterance contains a vague quantifier and the question asks how many or how likely, treat it as numeric estimation that produces a quantity, not pragmatic meaning.
- If the meaning is carried by a glance or gesture rather than words, treat it as a nonverbal-cue problem.
- If the words plainly fit the situation with no mismatch, answer literally rather than inventing a hidden meaning.

## Generalization Note
Use this macro as the reusable prototype for decoding non-literal utterances the speaker wants understood: indirect requests and hints, irony and sarcasm (opposite-of-words), humor (non-serious incongruity), and softened complaints, warnings, refusals, and reminders. The decisive test is a mismatch between literal wording and intended, honest meaning; the micro expansions are only for finer diagnosis, motive analysis, or boundary explanation.
