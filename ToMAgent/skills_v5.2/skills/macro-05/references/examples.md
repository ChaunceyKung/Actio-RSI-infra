# macro-05 Examples and Boundaries

## Decision Variable
why a character's true feeling differs from outward display or ordinary expectation

## Route Signal
Either a mismatch between what a character shows and what they truly feel (concealed, faked, or reversed), or a reaction that is surprising only because it is judged against the character's own stated goal or desire instead of the observer's default.

## Hard Boundary
- Moral emotion and responsibility judgment
- Appearance-reality and knowledge boundary
- Social cue and nonverbal intention
- Indirect language and conversational meaning

## Shortcut To Avoid
- Do not appraise the event from your own default reaction; appraise it against the character's stated goal or desire (a 'nice' gift can still disappoint someone who wanted something specific).
- Do not read the visible expression as the felt emotion when the question asks how the character truly feels; check for a motive to mask first.
- Do not convert the problem into blame or fault just because the scene contains harm or awkwardness.

## Common Failure Modes
- Defaulting to the observer's typical reaction (a bicycle is great, so 'happy') instead of measuring the outcome against the character's specific prior wish.
- Ignoring the character's stated prior desire or expectation, which is the very thing that makes the reaction atypical.
- Conflating the shown display with the felt emotion, taking a polite smile or cheerful act at face value when it is concealment.
- Forcing an 'atypical' or 'hidden' reading onto a plain scene that has no conflicting desire and no reason to mask.
- Drifting into moral judgment or blame when the question only asks what the character feels.

## Minimal Pair
- Case: A child hopes for a computer or a new game for his birthday and instead receives a bicycle: the felt emotion is disappointment. | Why: The outcome is appraised against the character's own stated wish, so a perfectly nice gift falls short and produces an atypical reaction; this is exactly the desire-relative inference this macro handles.
- Case: The same child simply receives a bicycle for his birthday, with no prior wish mentioned: the felt emotion is happiness. | Why: Identical event, but with no conflicting personal goal the ordinary appraisal holds and the typical reaction is correct; it shows the decisive variable is the character's stated desire, not the gift, and warns against over-applying 'atypical'.
- Case: A cleaner who unintentionally throws away a student's painting feels guilty once he realizes the harm he caused. | Why: Here the emotion is driven by a responsibility-and-harm appraisal rather than by masking or a desire-relative surprise, so it belongs to moral-emotion reasoning, not to this true-feeling-versus-display macro.

## Boundary Stress Test
- If a gift, grade, or outcome looks good by ordinary standards, check whether the character had a specific prior wish it fails to meet before assuming the typical positive emotion.
- If the character acts cheerful or calm, test whether there is a motive to mask (wanting permission, politeness, protecting someone) before equating the display with the felt emotion.
- If two opposite feelings could both be true (happy for a friend, sad to lose), keep the mixed emotion rather than collapsing to one.
- If the scene includes harm, fault, or someone being helped, test whether the emotion is really about responsibility and norms (guilt, pride, gratitude) rather than a hidden or desire-relative feeling.

## Generalization Note
This macro generalizes across cases where the solver must recover private emotion from social masking, emotional reversal, or atypical appraisal without relying on a single fixed pattern.
