# Persuasion, Advice, and Audience Calibration

## Use When
- Use when one person must move a specific listener toward a fixed goal (change a belief, attitude, or behavior), and the persuader's goal stays the same while only the listener is meant to move.
- Use when the task is to pick the best line, advice, framing, or angle for that listener among options.
- Use when the listener has voiced a specific objection or concern (it is too noisy, too risky, I do not trust you, I am busy) and the answer must address that exact concern.
- Use when the right answer depends on the listener's knowledge, motive, resistance, trust, values, emotional state, or relationship to the persuader rather than on the objectively most complete argument.

## Do Not Use When
- Do not use when both parties must give something up to reach a joint plan and the answer is the deal they settle on; that is two-way bargaining, not one-way influence.
- Do not use when the two people simply want different things and the question is whose preference gets accommodated, since there the actor moves himself to please the other rather than persuading the other.
- Do not use when the core issue is only decoding an indirect or implied line, judging truth versus deception, or reading literal meaning, with no listener to be moved.
- Do not use when the answer turns on quantity, probability, or estimating from a sample rather than on adapting a message to a listener.

## Decision Variable
which message moves this specific listener toward the persuader's fixed goal by answering the listener's stated concern while staying true to the persuader's real motive

## Direct Route Rule
Route immediately when the scene asks how one person should phrase, frame, or argue something to move a specific listener toward a goal the persuader keeps fixed, and the listener has a concern the message must address.

## Trigger Checklist
- There is a persuader with a fixed goal and a specific target listener.
- The persuader's aim is to change the listener's belief, attitude, or behavior toward that goal, not to settle a deal or to accommodate the listener's different wish.
- The listener has a stated barrier or objection: low knowledge, skepticism, fear, pride, mistrust, a competing priority, or a specific worry they named.
- The answer is a choice among candidate lines or strategies, and the best one is the one tailored to this listener.
- Winning depends on matching the appeal to the listener's concern (a sincere personal appeal for feelings or values, concrete proof for doubts about feasibility or evidence), not on the most complete or most literal answer.
- Neither does the scene require both sides to trade concessions, nor is the real task to decode an implied meaning.

## Workflow
- Fix the persuader's goal and keep it fixed. State the one outcome the persuader actually wants (a belief, attitude, or behavior change). The best answer moves the listener toward THIS goal; it does not quietly switch to a different goal.
- Find the listener's single most specific stated concern or objection. Read the scene for the exact worry, value, or barrier the listener voiced (for example: it is too noisy, the parts will make a mess, the plan is not feasible, I do not trust you, I am busy tonight). This concern is the target the winning answer must hit.
- Identify the persuader's genuine motive and the relationship. Name why the persuader truly wants this (a real personal desire, care for the listener, a fair benefit) and how close or formal the relationship is. The strongest answer is honest about that real motive rather than inventing a fake reason.
- Select the option that directly resolves the listener's stated concern while staying true to the persuader's real goal and motive. Prefer the answer that speaks to the exact worry the listener raised.
- Match the appeal type to what the concern is made of, do not default to one register. If the concern is emotional or relational (fear, pride, love, feeling disrespected, a close family tie), the calibrated move is a sincere personal or relational appeal, not a technical argument. If the concern is factual or about feasibility, risk, or evidence (a skeptical expert, a doubtful buyer, a teacher who thinks the student is lying), the calibrated move is concrete proof, a demonstration, or a credible example, not a bare 'just trust me'.
- Reject these four kinds of weak answers: (a) an over-engineered or over-rational argument aimed at a concern the listener does not actually hold; (b) an answer that abandons or swaps the persuader's goal (offering a different activity, a compromise, or 'we can do it your way instead') when the aim is to win the listener over to the original goal - that is negotiation, not persuasion; (c) any answer that ignores the listener's stated objection and gives a generic appeal to a generic audience; (d) any answer that relies on dishonesty, manipulation, coercion, threats, or going over the listener's head - effective, trust-preserving persuasion stays honest and listener-respecting.
- If the scene instead asks why a particular line was said, infer the influence goal behind it: which of the listener's concerns it was tuned to and what change in the listener it was meant to produce.

## Special Case
**Resistance is emotional or relational, so an over-rational answer loses**: When the listener's resistance is rooted in feelings, values, or the relationship rather than in missing facts, the winning answer is a sincere personal or relational appeal that names the persuader's genuine desire, not the most technical or fact-dense option. Reserve evidence-and-demonstration answers for listeners whose objection is actually about feasibility, risk, or proof.

Scene: A child really wants to go to the amusement park today. Her father resists, giving one specific reason: he thinks the park is too noisy.
Question: Which line best persuades the father to go?
Answer logic: Two tempting wrong answers are an over-engineered technical fix for the noise (citing soundproof walls and quieter equipment) and a compromise that abandons the goal (suggesting they go to a park or zoo instead). The strongest answer is the child honestly expressing her genuine, strong personal wish, that she has not been in a long time and it means a lot to her. It keeps the persuader's real goal fixed, it is authentic to her true motive, and it speaks to a father-child relationship where a heartfelt wish carries more weight than a technical rebuttal of his stated noise concern. Over-solving the literal objection or deflecting to a different activity both miss what actually moves this listener.

## Boundary Exit Rule
- If both parties must give something up to reach a joint plan, and the answer is the deal or allocation they settle on, this is two-way bargaining, not one-way persuasion. Solve it by finding the mutually acceptable trade where each side concedes, instead of trying to move only one listener toward a fixed goal.
- If the two people simply want different things and the question is which person's preference will be accommodated (for example, an outdoor lover inviting a homebody and choosing where to go), the task is to predict whose desire wins, not to craft a persuasive message. Answer by reasoning about whose preference the actor defers to, not by composing a pitch.
- If the key issue is only what an indirect or implied line really means, with no audience to be moved, this is decoding implied meaning. Reason about the speaker's intended meaning behind the literal words instead of choosing the most effective message.
- If the main task is to judge whether a statement is true, false, or misleading rather than how to make it land with a listener, this is truth and deception evaluation. Assess the statement's truth status instead of tailoring it for influence.
- If the answer hinges on quantity, probability, or estimating from a sample rather than on moving a listener, this is a quantitative or sampling judgment, not persuasion. Reason about the numbers instead of the audience.
