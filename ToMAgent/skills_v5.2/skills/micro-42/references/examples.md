# micro-42 Examples and Boundaries

## Decision Variable
whether a remark or act breaches the tact of its setting and is likely to embarrass, hurt, or offend someone present

## Route Signal
Route here when the question is 'did anyone say something inappropriate' or 'which sentence was inappropriate' — i.e. judging a remark or act for tact, embarrassment, or offense toward someone present.

## Hard Boundary
- Not audience calibration: simplifying or tailoring a message to a listener is appropriate, not a tact breach. Dismissively refusing to help a confused listener, however, IS a faux pas.
- Not harm and responsibility: the issue is whether the remark was tactless, not who caused harm or who is to blame.
- Not repair: the issue is whether the act was a faux pas, not how to apologize for it.
- Not non-literal comprehension: judging tact is different from inferring why a sarcastic remark was said.

## Shortcut To Avoid
- Do not treat any awkward or uncomfortable scene as automatically containing a faux pas; a normal compliment, request, or polite reply is not inappropriate (some stories have no faux pas — the answer is 'no one').
- Do not let 'the remark is true' or 'the speaker meant no harm' override a clear tact breach.
- Do not slide from the tact verdict into assigning blame or proposing an apology when the prompt only asks what was inappropriate.

## Common Failure Modes
- Treating a blunt-but-true put-down as acceptable because it is honest, and answering 'no faux pas'.
- Over-calling: flagging a normal, polite line as inappropriate when the story actually contains no faux pas.
- Misreading audience calibration (simplifying for a novice) as a faux pas just because a listener is addressed.
- Confusing the tactless remark with the question of who is to blame or who should apologize, and routing to harm/repair.
- Picking the line that was hurt-BY the faux pas (the victim's reply) instead of the line that committed it.

## Minimal Pair
- Case: Xiaofang proudly shows her new blue curtains; her friend Xiaolan says 'the color is too ugly, you should buy new ones.' Q: which sentence is inappropriate? | Why: In-scope faux pas: a blunt put-down of something the listener is proud of, judged for tact.
- Case: Same curtains, but the friend says 'Wow, these are beautiful, they match your room well.' Q: which sentence is inappropriate? | Why: Routes to 'no faux pas': the matched control story is identical except the remark is a normal compliment, so the answer is 'no one said anything inappropriate.' Same decisive variable, opposite tact verdict.
- Case: A scientist explains a paper to middle-schoolers using simple analogies, avoiding jargon. Q: was that a faux pas? | Why: Routes to audience calibration, not a faux pas: tailoring wording to the listener is appropriate; nothing embarrasses or offends. Differs from the in-scope case only in that the adaptation helps rather than wounds the listener.

## Boundary Stress Test
- If the remark is honest but embarrasses someone present, it is still a faux pas — decide by tact, not truthfulness.
- If the story's lines are all normal compliments, requests, or polite replies, the correct answer is 'no one said anything inappropriate' — do not invent a faux pas.
- If the speaker simplifies or adapts wording to help a novice/child/outsider, that is audience calibration, not a faux pas.
- If the question asks who is to blame or whether an apology is owed, the task has shifted to harm or repair — exit.

## Generalization Note
Apply across any culture or setting by substituting the local decorum norm; the core question is always whether the remark crosses the setting's tact line and is likely to embarrass, hurt, or offend someone present — independent of whether it was true or intentional.
