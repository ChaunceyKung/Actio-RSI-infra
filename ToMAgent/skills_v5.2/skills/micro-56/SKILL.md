# Causal Attribution

## Use When
- Use when the question asks what person, event, or condition caused an outcome.
- Use when the task is to identify the causal source of a result, change, or event.
- Use when the scene asks what led to, triggered, produced, or made something happen.
- Use when the answer should name the cause even if no one is being judged.

## Do Not Use When
- Do not use when the main issue is who is at fault, responsible, guilty, or deserving blame.
- Do not use when the question is about moral evaluation, apology, fault assignment, or deserved punishment.
- Do not use when the task is predicting beliefs, intentions, emotions, or actions rather than naming a cause.
- Do not use when the main issue is probability, typicality, quantity update, or evidence strength instead of causation.

## Decision Variable
the cause of the result

## Trigger Checklist
- The question asks 'why' did this happen, or 'what caused / led to / produced' this outcome.
- It wants the factor that explains a result, often a surprising or unexpected one, not a moral verdict.
- The right answer can be a hidden condition, background fact, or accident, not necessarily a person who is at fault.
- If the question instead asks who is to blame, who should apologize, or who said the wrong thing, this is NOT the unit.

## Workflow
- Step 1: Name the outcome that needs explaining (often a surprising emotion or event: 'should feel X but feels Y').
- Step 2: Read the literal question. Confirm it asks for the CAUSE (why / what produced it), not who is to blame, who should apologize, or what someone intended to do on purpose. If it asks for blame or fault, stop and route to the harm-and-responsibility judgment instead.
- Step 3: Scan the scene and options for the factor that actually produces the outcome. It is whatever fills the gap between expectation and reality (e.g. he wanted a computer, not a bicycle; the brother sacrificed for the tuition).
- Step 4: Pick that causal factor even when it is an accident, a hidden background condition, or carries no blame. Do NOT upgrade it to a guilty party, a moral verdict, or an inferred intention.

## Special Case
**Surprising-outcome 'why' questions ask for the cause, not blame**: When a scene says someone should feel one way but feels another and asks why, the answer is the hidden fact or condition that explains the reaction. It is a causal explanation, not a judgment of who is good, bad, or at fault.

Scene: Xiao Ming receives a bicycle on his birthday. He should be very happy, but he is very disappointed.
Question: Why is he disappointed?
Answer logic: The cause is the gap between expectation and reality: he hoped for a computer, not a bicycle. Answer with that explaining condition. No one is being blamed, so do not reach for fault or apology language.

Non-emotion causal illustration (same operation, no surprising-feeling template): "An old footbridge collapsed during routine use. What caused it to collapse?" The cause is the physical factor that produced the failure: a corroded support bolt that finally gave way. That is the answer even though it is an accident with no one at fault and no emotion involved. Causal attribution covers physical and event-based 'why did this happen' questions, not only 'should feel X but feels Y' scenes; name the producing factor, not a culprit.

## Boundary Exit Rule
- PRIMARY confusion (cause vs blame): if the question asks who is at fault, guilty, responsible, should apologize, said the wrong thing, or deserves a moral verdict, that is a blame and social-fault judgment, not causal attribution; route to the harm-and-responsibility neighbor. Naming the factor that produced the outcome stays here; judging the person who produced it leaves.
- If the scene asks the immediate purpose behind an on-purpose act (why someone DID something deliberately), that is intention reading, handle it there, not here.
- If the main task is estimating how likely something is, how typical it is, or updating a quantity, route to the likelihood / quantity-estimation neighbor.
- If the focus is how new evidence shifts a person's belief, route to the belief-update neighbor rather than treating it as a cause-of-outcome question.

## Quick Route Signal
Use this unit when the prompt asks WHY an outcome happened or WHAT caused, produced, or led to it, especially a surprising result where someone 'should feel X but feels Y'. The answer is the explaining factor, not a verdict on who is to blame.
