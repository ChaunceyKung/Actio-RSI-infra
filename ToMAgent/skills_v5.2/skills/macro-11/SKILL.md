# Commitments, Plans, and Next Action

## Use When
- Use when the scene asks what the character does next, given that a prior commitment, plan, promise, appointment, or already-started task is in place.
- Use when an interruption, invitation, request, or new opportunity arrives before the planned or in-progress action is finished, and the question is which action happens next.
- Use when the answer turns on priority among actions of one agent: continue, resume, defer, decline, or switch, decided by what was promised, what is underway, social obligation, and time pressure.
- Use when, after the character handles an interruption, you must predict whether the original task resumes or is set aside.

## Do Not Use When
- Do not use when the question asks what a gesture, glance, hint, or topic shift MEANS, or what the character WANTS or is signaling; reading the purpose behind an indirect cue is a nonverbal-intention problem, not a next-action-under-commitment problem.
- Do not use when two or more people must agree on a shared choice and the puzzle is whose preference wins or how to split conflicting wants; that is a preference-conflict / joint-allocation problem.
- Do not use when the next step is driven by a simple dominant desire and the deal or detour that satisfies it, with no prior promise, duty, or in-progress task; tracking how a plain want gets satisfied is a desire-drives-action problem.
- Do not use when the core task is how to phrase a refusal or persuade a listener, or whether something was rude or embarrassing; those are communication and social-offense problems.

## Decision Variable
what the character does next when an existing commitment, plan, or in-progress task competes with a new opportunity or interruption

## Direct Route Rule
Route immediately when one agent already has a commitment, plan, or started task, a competing event appears, and the question asks for the concrete next action (continue, resume, defer, decline, or switch).

## Trigger Checklist
- A prior commitment, plan, promise, appointment, or in-progress task is already established before the new event.
- A new invitation, request, interruption, or opportunity appears before that planned or started action is finished.
- The question asks for the next concrete action, not what the character wants, feels, or is hinting at.
- The correct answer depends on whether the character continues, resumes, defers, declines, or switches.
- Priority is set by what was promised, what is underway, social obligation, and time pressure rather than by simple liking.

## Workflow
- Name the standing commitment or in-progress task: what was promised, scheduled, or already started, and how far along it is.
- Name the competing event: the interruption, request, invitation, or new opportunity, and exactly when it must be acted on (now vs. later).
- Weigh priority along four axes: an explicit promise or duty outranks a casual want; an already-started task carries continuation pressure; social obligation to a waiting person matters; time pressure or a deadline can force the order.
- Check whether the original commitment is still live or has been discharged: if someone credibly takes it over or it is genuinely finished, it no longer competes and the character is freed to take the new option.
- Predict the single concrete next action. If the interruption is now handled and the original task is still unfinished and uncovered, the character RESUMES it; do not silently drop a commitment the scene implies continues.
- Confirm the chosen action answers 'what does the character DO next', not 'what does the character want' and not 'how should this be phrased'.

## Special Case
**Resume after an interruption, unless relief is arranged**: When a character is pulled away from a started task or a standing plan, the default next action after the interruption is to resume that task or plan. The exception is when the scene shows the commitment is genuinely covered (someone reliable takes it over) or already complete, in which case the character is free to take the new opportunity.

Scene: Maya is halfway through preparing slides for a meeting she promised to lead when a friend texts asking to grab coffee right now. No one else can cover the slides.
Question: What does Maya do next?
Answer logic: The slides are an in-progress, promised task and no relief is arranged, while the coffee is only a fresh, deferrable opportunity, so she keeps preparing the slides and defers the coffee. Had a reliable colleague offered to finish the slides, the commitment would be covered and going for coffee would become the predicted next action.

## Boundary Exit Rule
- If the question asks what a glance, gesture, hint, or topic shift MEANS, or what the character WANTS or is trying to get someone to do, treat it as reading the purpose behind an indirect cue (a nonverbal-intention problem); infer the intended meaning of the signal rather than ranking competing actions.
- If two or more people must settle on a shared plan and the puzzle is whose preference should win or how to balance conflicting wants, treat it as a preference-conflict / joint-allocation problem; model each person's preference and find the choice that best reconciles them, not a single agent's next step.
- If the next step is set by a simple dominant desire plus the deal or detour that satisfies it, with no prior promise, duty, or started task, treat it as a desire-drives-action problem; follow how the plain want gets fulfilled through the chain of options.
- If the core task is choosing polite wording for a refusal or delay, or persuading a listener, treat it as a communication problem; pick the phrasing that fits the listener and goal rather than deciding which action happens.
- If the question is whether something was rude, embarrassing, or offensive, treat it as a social-offense problem; judge the breach and any repair rather than the next action.
- If the answer hinges on who can see or reach what from where, treat it as a spatial-perspective problem; reason about viewpoint and access rather than action priority.

## Quick Route Signal
The character already has a plan, promise, appointment, or task underway, and a new option appears that could interrupt or redirect them; the question asks what they DO next.
