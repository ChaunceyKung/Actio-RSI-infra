# Indirect Language and Conversational Meaning

## Use When
- The speaker's literal words and their intended meaning come apart, and the task is to infer what the speaker really wants the listener to understand.
- The utterance is an indirect request or hint, irony or sarcasm (meaning the opposite of the words), humor (a non-serious incongruity), or a softened complaint, warning, refusal, or reminder.
- The key question is the intended meaning or communicative purpose of an utterance the speaker genuinely wants understood, not the literal sentence meaning.

## Do Not Use When
- Do not use when the central issue is whether the statement is true and whether the speaker intends to deceive; a lie, white lie, or misdirection is a truth-value and intent-to-deceive problem, where you judge if the words are false and why the speaker would say something untrue, not what honest-but-indirect meaning to decode.
- Do not use when the task is estimating HOW MANY or HOW LIKELY from a vague quantifier (for example 'almost every', 'some', 'a few'), a base rate, or a partial sample; that is numeric/base-rate estimation that yields a quantity, not pragmatic meaning, and it belongs to quantity-estimation reasoning.
- Do not use when the carrier of meaning is a nonverbal cue such as a gesture, glance, posture, or facial expression rather than a spoken or written utterance; reasoning about body language and looks is a separate nonverbal-cue problem.
- Do not use when the main issue is the speaker's hidden emotion for its own sake, rather than the communicative act the utterance performs.
- Do not use when the core task is choosing how to persuade, advise, or tailor a message to a particular listener, rather than decoding an utterance that has already been said.
- Do not use when the scene is a plain literal statement that means exactly what it says, with no mismatch between wording and context; do not over-apply indirect inference where none is signaled.

## Decision Variable
what the speaker really wants the listener to understand from an utterance whose literal wording differs from its intended meaning

## Direct Route Rule
Route immediately when an utterance's literal wording does not match what the speaker clearly wants understood, so the listener must decode an indirect request or hint, irony or sarcasm, or humor.

## Expand With Micro Units
- Literal Meaning: the sentence's explicit semantic content (micro-34)
- Indirect Speech Act: what the utterance is doing socially (micro-35)
- Conversational Implicature: the implicit information derived from context (micro-36)
- Speaker Motivation: the speaker's social motive for choosing that utterance form (micro-39)

## Trigger Checklist
- There is a visible mismatch between the literal words and the situation, so taking the words at face value would be odd or pointless.
- The scene asks for the intended meaning, communicative purpose, or 'real meaning' of the utterance, not its literal sentence meaning.
- The utterance works as an indirect request or hint, as irony or sarcasm meaning the opposite of the words, or as a joke that is a non-serious incongruity.
- The listener must infer what is meant from context, relationship, tone, or shared expectations.
- The speaker genuinely wants this meaning understood; the question is not whether the words are true or false.

## Workflow
- State the literal utterance and what it would mean if taken at face value.
- Check the context: why would the speaker say exactly this, here, now? Detect the mismatch between the literal wording and the situation, relationship, or shared expectations.
- If the literal reading fits the situation with no mismatch, stop and answer literally; do not invent a hidden meaning.
- If there is a mismatch, identify which non-literal act it is: a HINT or indirect request prompts the listener to do or notice something; IRONY or sarcasm means the opposite of the words, usually to criticize or complain; HUMOR is an obvious non-serious incongruity meant to amuse.
- Infer the intended message from that act, using context and relationship, and prefer it over the literal wording.
- Sanity check that the speaker WANTS this meaning understood (it is honest and communicative), not that the speaker is hiding a falsehood or estimating a quantity.
- State the intended meaning in plain language as the answer.

## Special Case
**Irony and sarcasm: the speaker means the opposite**: When the literal words are clearly contradicted by the situation (praise after a failure, 'great weather' in a downpour), read the utterance as irony or sarcasm: the intended meaning is the OPPOSITE of the literal words, usually a complaint, criticism, or mockery the speaker wants understood.

Scene: Anming ignores the meal his mother spent a long time preparing, never looking up or saying thank you. His mother says angrily, 'You are really polite!'
Question: Why does the mother say this?
Answer logic: The literal words praise Anming's manners, but the situation (rudeness, anger) contradicts them, so this is sarcasm. The intended meaning is the opposite: she is mocking Anming for being ill-mannered. The answer is the ironic criticism, not the literal compliment.

## Boundary Exit Rule
- If the central question is whether the statement is true and whether the speaker means to deceive (a lie, white lie, or honest mistake), solve it as a truth-value-and-intent problem: judge whether the words are false and why the speaker would say something untrue, rather than decoding an indirect-but-honest meaning here.
- If the task is to estimate how many or how likely from a vague quantifier, base rate, or partial sample, solve it as numeric estimation that combines the prior with the observed evidence to produce a quantity, not as pragmatic meaning.
- If the meaning is carried by a gesture, glance, posture, or facial expression rather than an utterance, reason about the nonverbal cue directly: what that body signal conveys in context.
- If the central issue is the speaker's hidden feeling for its own sake rather than the act the utterance performs, treat it as an emotion-inference problem.
- If the core task is how to persuade, advise, or tailor a message to a listener rather than decode an utterance already spoken, treat it as a persuasion or audience-adaptation problem.

## Quick Route Signal
A speaker says something whose literal wording clearly does not match the situation, and the listener is expected to infer the real intended meaning: an indirect request or hint, an ironic or sarcastic reversal, or a joke.
