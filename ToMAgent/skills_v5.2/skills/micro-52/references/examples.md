# micro-52 Examples and Boundaries

## Decision Variable
the communication move that best fits the power gap

## Route Signal
Route here when the scene asks WHICH MOVE someone should make across a power/authority gap — comply, defer, ask permission, push back, escalate, delegate, challenge, or retreat. If it only asks who outranks whom, route to identifying the power structure instead.

## Hard Boundary
- It CHOOSES the move; if the question only asks who outranks whom (naming the role/rank structure), it belongs with identifying the power structure, not here.
- Broader than one-way persuasion: persuasion changes a belief or attitude; this unit picks any coping move (comply, defer, escalate, delegate, retreat), and it specifically requires a power gap.
- Not audience calibration: that adjusts the wording for the listener's level; this picks the action, which can be the same words sent through a different channel.
- Not negotiation between equals: no bilateral trade-off is required, and the deciding factor is rank, not mutual concession.

## Shortcut To Avoid
- Do not answer from fairness or 'who is right' alone without checking who holds authority and which direction the actor is speaking.
- Do not treat naming the rank as the answer; the answer is the move the actor makes given that rank.
- Do not assume more power means a more forceful move; a superior asking a subordinate is often best served by a plain direct request, not by invoking rank.
- Do not confuse respectful wording with the strategic choice of action.

## Common Failure Modes
- Answering with the role/rank ('the manager has authority') when the question asks what the actor should DO about it.
- Picking the option that leans on the actor's effort or loyalty when speaking up, instead of substance the superior can evaluate.
- Choosing to route around the authority figure (other colleagues, going over their head) when a normal direct channel is open.
- Overusing blunt direct challenge upward when an evidence-based or procedural move is safer and more effective.
- Solving the scene as compromise/negotiation when the hierarchy, not mutual trade-off, constrains the options.

## Minimal Pair
- Case: A new hire is introduced as operations lead; the question asks who now has decision authority over the team. | Why: NO match — this only asks to name the rank structure (who outranks whom), with no move to choose. It belongs with identifying the power/role structure, not here. This is the primary confusion to avoid.
- Case: An engineer's plan is rejected by his leader and he must decide how to respond — push back with evidence, plead loyalty, or go around the leader. | Why: MATCH — the question asks which coping move to make across an authority gap, the core of this unit.
- Case: Two peers disagree on dividing a shared project and must reach a workable split. | Why: NO match — equals seeking a compromise; bilateral negotiation, no power gap deciding the move.

## Boundary Stress Test
- Does the question ask what the actor should DO (a move), or only WHO is in charge (a label)? Only the first is this unit.
- If the same words sent to a boss, a peer, or a subordinate would change which move is best, the power gap is decisive and this unit applies.
- Strip the power gap and make the two parties equals: if the right move changes, power was load-bearing (this unit); if it does not, it was plain persuasion or negotiation.

## Generalization Note
This skill generalizes to workplace, family, institutional, and status-based interactions where the key question is how to communicate strategically under hierarchy.
