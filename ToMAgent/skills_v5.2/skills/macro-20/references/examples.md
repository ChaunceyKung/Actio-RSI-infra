# macro-20 Examples and Boundaries

## Decision Variable
which communication action is most appropriate given the direction and size of the power gap, the stakes, and the norms of the setting

## Route Signal
A status difference changes which message, tone, channel, or escalation step is appropriate, and the question is how to communicate across that gap.

## Hard Boundary
- Not a peer-negotiation problem when only the higher- or lower-ranked party can realistically decide the move and rank constrains it.
- Not a self-presentation problem when the central choice is how to address someone above or below in rank, not how one looks to an audience.
- Not a pure persuasion problem when the rank itself rules some otherwise-convincing moves out as too blunt or insubordinate.
- Not a role-identification problem when the role is already known but the communication move is still undecided.

## Shortcut To Avoid
- Do not default to the most polite or deferential option when the stakes actually demand a clear challenge or escalation.
- Do not treat the hierarchy as decorative background; the direction and size of the power gap can decide the correct action.
- Do not flatten upward, downward, and peer communication into one generic politeness rule.
- Do not pick the most persuasive-sounding argument while ignoring that rank makes that move risky or insubordinate.

## Common Failure Modes
- Ignoring the power asymmetry and answering as if the parties were equals.
- Choosing a move too blunt for the status gap, such as a direct public challenge to a senior when private or formal channels were called for.
- Choosing a move too timid for the stakes, deferring or staying silent when a serious issue warranted pushing back or escalating.
- Going around the chain of command when a direct, calibrated move would have worked, or arguing in place when escalation was the only remaining channel.
- Confusing managing one's own image with navigating authority, and answering as if likeability rather than rank were the deciding variable.

## Minimal Pair
- Case: A junior engineer whose proposal his leader rejected must decide how to make the case again across that rank gap. | Why: Rank rules out pleading effort or lobbying behind the leader's back; the right move is the upward one that respects the leader's authority while presenting hard evidence.
- Case: A speaker preparing how to come across at an event so the audience forms a good impression of them. | Why: The deciding variable is the speaker's self-image to an audience, not a move calibrated to who outranks whom, so this is self-presentation rather than power-gap communication.

## Boundary Stress Test
- A subordinate who knows the boss is wrong must decide whether to say so in private, in public, or by email, weighing rank against the seriousness of the error.
- A junior doctor must decide whether to question a senior's instruction immediately, in front of others, or to escalate quietly after the shift.
- An employee whose direct manager keeps dismissing a real risk must decide whether to keep pressing or report it to someone higher.
- A new hire must decide how much to defer to a senior's flawed plan versus respectfully flag the flaw in their first week.

## Generalization Note
This prototype generalizes across workplace, family, school, service, medical, military, legal, and institutional settings: whenever a power or status gap is the variable that decides whether to defer, push back, challenge, concede, escalate, or go over someone's head.
