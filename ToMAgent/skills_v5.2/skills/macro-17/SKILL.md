# Apology, Compensation, and Reconciliation

## Use When
- Use when a hurtful act, insult, exclusion, betrayal, broken promise, or accidental damage has ALREADY occurred and someone is now hurt or angry.
- Use when the task is to choose the best repair move: acknowledge the harm, apologize, offer concrete amends or compensation, and reassure that it will not recur.
- Use when the right answer must restore trust and continued interaction, not merely state facts or win the point.
- Use when the scene is a live repair attempt by the person who caused (or is associated with) the harm.

## Do Not Use When
- Do not use when no real harm has occurred yet and the task is to clarify a live misunderstanding before anyone is hurt; that is pre-damage clarification, where the move is to restate intent, not to apologize.
- Do not use when the only question is WHETHER (or which sentence) something inappropriate was said; that is detecting an offense, a judgment task, not performing the repair.
- Do not use when both sides are bargaining over terms or building a joint plan from conflicting preferences; that calls for trading concessions, not making amends.
- Do not use when the central evidence is a long history of trustworthiness, reputation, or loyalty rather than a single offense to repair right now.
- Do not use when the scene is mainly about managing one's image, first impression, or formal etiquette with no relationship injury to mend.

## Decision Variable
given that harm has occurred, which repair move (acknowledgement, apology, amends, prevention) is proportional to the injury and best restores the relationship

## Direct Route Rule
Route immediately when an offense or harm has already happened and the scene asks how to apologize, make amends, or restore the relationship.

## Trigger Checklist
- A specific person was hurt, excluded, insulted, embarrassed, betrayed, or let down by an act that has already happened.
- The actor (or someone responsible) must now decide how to respond to repair the bond.
- The scene is about restoring the relationship, not establishing the facts or winning an argument.
- A repair gesture is expected: admitting the harm, expressing remorse, making restitution, and/or promising changed behavior.
- The best answer is the one that most credibly reopens trust and continued interaction.

## Workflow
- Confirm real harm actually occurred and identify WHO was harmed. If no one is hurt and it is only a misread or a still-correctable misunderstanding, do not apologize; clarify intent instead.
- Gauge the severity of the harm and the actor's degree of responsibility (deliberate, careless, or accidental; minor slight or serious betrayal). This sets how much repair is owed.
- Acknowledge the harm explicitly and from the victim's point of view, before any explanation. Naming the impact ('I hurt you / I damaged this') must come first.
- Apologize sincerely and take responsibility; do not defend, justify ('I was only being honest'), or shift blame, which deepens the rupture.
- Add amends proportional to the injury: concrete restitution or compensation for material harm, reassurance and changed behavior for emotional harm, and a credible step to prevent recurrence for repeated or serious harm.
- Calibrate the size of the repair to the injury: do not over-repair a trivial slight (which looks insincere) nor under-repair a serious wound with a hollow apology and no amends.
- Return the option that combines acknowledgement + apology + proportional amends and most plausibly restores trust and ongoing interaction.

## Special Case
**Repair after accidental harm**: When the harm was accidental, lighter blame language is fine, but the repair move is still primary: acknowledge the damage, apologize, and offer to make it right. An accident does not excuse skipping the apology or the amends; denying or minimizing it (e.g., blaming someone else) makes the rupture worse.

Scene: A friend accidentally knocks over and breaks another friend's favorite mug, and the friend is clearly upset.
Question: What should the person do next?
Answer logic: Real harm occurred and the friend is the victim (step 1); it was an accident but the actor is responsible (step 2); so acknowledge the damage from the friend's view, apologize sincerely, and offer to replace the mug or otherwise make it right (steps 3-5). Denying it ('the dog did it'), or explaining it away without an apology or replacement, fails because it leaves the relationship unrepaired.

## Boundary Exit Rule
- If no harm has happened yet and the scene is a still-fixable misunderstanding, treat it as pre-damage clarification: restate what was actually meant to prevent harm, rather than apologizing for harm that has not occurred.
- If the only question is whether, or which sentence, someone said something inappropriate, that is detecting an offense (a judgment about what happened), not repairing it; answer the detection question and do not jump to an apology move.
- If both sides are negotiating terms, concessions, or a shared plan from conflicting preferences, the task is to find a mutually acceptable trade-off, not to make amends for an injury.
- If the decisive evidence is a long record of trustworthiness, reputation, or loyalty across many interactions, the task is judging the relationship over time, not executing a single repair now.
- If the core issue is managing image, formality, or rank-sensitive self-presentation with no relationship injury, the task is impression management, not reconciliation after offense.
