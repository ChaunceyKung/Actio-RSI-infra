# Preference Conflict and Joint Decision

## Use When
- Use when the scene asks what action, choice, or allocation should be selected once different parties want different outcomes.
- Use when two or more people want different things and the key question is which option gets chosen, not how the bargaining unfolds.
- Use when a shared decision, a split of a resource, or the next move must be determined from competing wants or stated priorities.
- Use when one agent holds two competing desires and you must predict the action that follows the dominant one (even if it contradicts a desire the agent states out loud).
- Use when the outcome turns on preference strength, stated priority, ownership, role, who is hosting or accommodating whom, or fairness/reciprocity under conflict.

## Do Not Use When
- Do not use when the task is how one party should move or convince another while keeping their own goal fixed (one-way persuasion): there the answer is the best persuasive move, not which preference wins.
- Do not use when both sides actively trade concessions to co-build a joint plan and the question is about that give-and-take itself, rather than picking one resulting outcome.
- Do not use when the main question is whether an utterance is true, deceptive, or misleading.
- Do not use when the main question is what happens next purely because of a prior commitment, schedule, or invitation, with no real clash of preferences.
- Do not use when the main question is whether a remark caused embarrassment, offense, or social harm.

## Decision Variable
which action, option, or allocation should be chosen when preferences conflict (or which competing desire wins inside one agent)

## Direct Route Rule
Route immediately when two parties want different outcomes, or one agent holds competing desires, and the question asks which choice, allocation, or action results.

## Expand With Micro Units
- Self Preference: the character's own preference (micro-19)
- Other Preference: the character's representation of another person's preference (micro-20)
- Goal Hierarchy: the means-end (nesting) relation among goals: which goal is superordinate (the end) and which is subordinate (a step serving it) (micro-21)
- Action Prediction: what the character is most likely to do next (micro-23)
- Goal-Conflict Arbitration: the weighting rule for competing goals: which active goal the character gives greater weight, and on what basis (micro-25)
- Fairness and Reciprocity: whether the give-and-take in this already-arranged exchange is balanced and reciprocal, or one-sidedly favors one party (micro-44)

## Trigger Checklist
- Two or more parties want different outcomes, OR one agent holds two competing desires of its own.
- The question asks for a choice, an allocation, or a predicted action, not for advice on how to convince someone.
- The answer depends on whose preference has priority, how strong each preference is, or which desire dominates an agent.
- The scene is about the resulting outcome of the conflict, not about the detailed back-and-forth that produced it.
- A shared decision, a split of something, or a single next action must be settled.

## Workflow
- Enumerate every preference in play. For an interpersonal scene, list each party and exactly what that party wants. For a single agent, list each of its competing desires. Tag each preference with its strength or stated priority (strongly wants, mildly prefers, only a passing thought).
- Pin down precisely what the question asks for: which option is chosen, who gets the resource, or what action the agent takes next. Answer that, not a related sub-question.
- Find the deciding rule the SCENE itself supplies and apply it. Common rules: (a) standing/courtesy, when one party hosts, invites, or gives to another, the outcome accommodates the recipient's preference, not the host's own taste; (b) stated priority or strength, the preference the story marks as stronger or more urgent wins; (c) ownership or role, whoever owns the thing or holds the deciding role controls it; (d) fairness or reciprocity, a return of an earlier favor or an even split when the story flags fairness.
- Track the RIGHT party. A frequent trap is reporting the preference of whoever is more vividly described while the question is about the other party's chosen outcome, do not let the more colorful actor's taste override the rule the scene actually applies.
- For a single agent that says one thing but wants another (a desire-action contradiction), the spoken denial is not the action. Predict the action that follows the DOMINANT desire and read off the scene's specific reason the stronger motive overrides the stated one, do not invent a generic-sounding excuse the story never gives.
- Only choose an even split or compromise when the scene actually signals fairness, indifference on both sides, or an explicit agreement to share. Do not default to a 50/50 compromise when one preference clearly dominates.
- State the final chosen action, allocation, or predicted move directly.

## Special Case
**Who has standing decides the outcome (host accommodates guest)**: When one party invites, hosts, or gives something to another, the scene usually resolves toward the RECIPIENT's preference out of courtesy, even though the host's own taste is described more vividly. Do not pick the host's favorite just because it is stated first or more strongly.

Scene: Liang loves outdoor adventure and hikes every weekend; Wenfang is a quiet librarian who prefers calm, indoor reading. Liang invites Wenfang to spend the weekend together.
Question: Where does Liang plan to spend the weekend with Wenfang?
Answer logic: Liang is the host issuing the invitation, so the standing rule applies: the outcome accommodates the guest Wenfang's preference for a quiet, indoor setting (the library), not Liang's own love of hiking. Picking hiking is the classic error of tracking the more vividly described actor's taste instead of the party the scene gives priority to.

## Boundary Exit Rule
- If the task is how one character should phrase, frame, or argue to win the other over while keeping their own goal fixed, that is one-way persuasion: the answer is the most effective persuasive move, so reason about what would shift the listener, not which preference is selected.
- If both sides actively trade concessions (each gives something to get something) to assemble a joint plan, and the question is about that mutual give-and-take, treat it as a negotiation: model the exchange of concessions and the agreement reached, rather than declaring one fixed preference the winner.
- If the task is whether someone is lying, half-telling, or misleading in their wording, solve it as a truthfulness judgment about the utterance, not as a choice between preferences.
- If what happens next is driven purely by a prior promise, schedule, or invitation with no genuine clash of wants, solve it as commitment follow-through: the binding obligation, not a preference, fixes the action.
- If the scene is mainly about embarrassment, offense, or a face threat caused by a remark, solve it as a social-harm judgment, not as a preference-conflict outcome.
