# Faux Pas, Offense, and Social Embarrassment

## Use When
- Use when the central question is whether anyone said or did something inappropriate, hurtful, embarrassing, or offensive in the scene.
- Use for the classic faux-pas structure: a speaker unintentionally says something that wounds or embarrasses a listener because the speaker did not know a relevant fact (for example, that the listener cared deeply about the thing being dismissed, or was personally connected to the group being criticized).
- Use when a remark, disclosure, or action may violate privacy, tact, or social comfort and the task is to judge whether it was inappropriate, not how to fix it.
- Use also when the answer may be that NOTHING inappropriate was said; deciding 'no faux pas' is part of this scene type.

## Do Not Use When
- Do not use when an offense has already been identified and the task is how to repair it: apologize, restore trust, or reconcile. That requires planning a corrective social move, not detecting whether harm occurred.
- Do not use when the task is to judge whether a statement is literally true, false, or a lie, including a kind white lie. That is truthfulness evaluation: compare the statement to reality and the speaker's belief, not to the listener's feelings.
- Do not use when the task is to recover an indirect or implied meaning, hint, request, or sarcasm. That requires inferring what the speaker meant beneath the surface, not whether the surface remark caused offense.
- Do not use when the task is to name which emotion a wrongdoer or victim feels afterward, such as guilt, shame, or embarrassment. That is appraising an emotional reaction to an act, not detecting whether the act itself was a social misstep.
- Do not use when the dominant issue is rank, hierarchy, or deference, or when it is preference conflict, next-action choice, or spatial perspective rather than social harm.

## Decision Variable
whether anyone in the scene said or did something inappropriate that hurts, embarrasses, or threatens another person's face, especially when the speaker was unaware of a fact that makes the remark hurtful

## Direct Route Rule
Route immediately when the question is whether a remark or act was inappropriate, awkward, or offensive, or whether a speaker committed a social blunder by saying something they would not have said had they known what the listener knows.

## Trigger Checklist
- The question asks 'does anyone say something inappropriate' or whether a remark or act was offensive, awkward, or embarrassing.
- A character makes a remark that could wound or embarrass another, often a comment that dismisses, criticizes, or exposes something the listener cares about.
- There is a fact the speaker plausibly did not know (the listener's hidden disappointment, illness, family connection, or secret) that turns an ordinary remark into a hurtful one.
- A private or sensitive detail is revealed in front of an audience that should not have heard it.
- The task is to detect whether a social misstep occurred, not to fix it, not to score its truth, and not to name the resulting emotion.

## Workflow
- Read every line of dialogue and action and find any remark or act that a listener could plausibly find hurtful, embarrassing, insulting, or exposing. If none exists, the answer is that no one said anything inappropriate; stop here.
- For each candidate remark, ask: would it hurt or embarrass the person it is about or directed at? If it is plainly neutral, kind, or a tactful compliment, it is not a faux pas.
- Apply the speaker-ignorance test: identify what the listener knows or feels (for example, that they badly wanted to win, are ill, or are connected to the group being criticized) and ask whether the speaker knew it. If the speaker did NOT know this fact and would not have made the remark had they known, this is a classic faux pas: the speaker blundered without intending harm.
- If both hold, a hurtful remark plus a speaker ignorant of the fact that makes it hurtful, answer that someone did say something inappropriate, and, if asked, name that remark as the faux pas.
- Distinguish deliberate rudeness from a faux pas: if the speaker fully knew the sensitive fact and chose to say the wounding thing anyway, it is intentional offense or an insult, not the classic faux pas (the listener may still be hurt, but the speaker was not ignorant).
- Do not be pulled into repair, truth-scoring, hidden-meaning, or emotion-naming. Answer only whether something inappropriate was said and, when relevant, why it counts (or does not count) as a faux pas.

## Special Case
**Speaker is ignorant of the fact that makes the remark hurtful**: The defining mark of a classic faux pas is that the speaker did not know something the listener knows, and that missing knowledge is exactly what turns an ordinary remark into a wounding one. The speaker is not malicious; they simply spoke without realizing. When the question is whether a faux pas occurred, the decisive checks are (a) would the remark hurt or embarrass the listener, and (b) did the speaker lack the knowledge that would have stopped them.

Scene: All fifth-graders enter a composition contest. Leo desperately wants to win but is absent the day results are posted, so he never learns that Hana won. On Monday Hana, the winner, greets Leo first and says, "I read your composition, it's not bad, just a bit of a shame." Leo is startled. Hana does not know how much Leo wanted to win.
Question: Does anyone say something inappropriate, and if so what makes it a faux pas?
Answer logic: Yes. Hana's 'just a bit of a shame' belittles the work of a rival who longed to win, so it would hurt Leo. Crucially, Hana does not know Leo cared so much, and she would not have made the dismissive remark if she did. A hurtful remark made in ignorance of the very fact that makes it hurtful is the classic faux pas, even though Hana intended no harm.

## Boundary Exit Rule
- If the offense is already established and the task is how to apologize, rebuild trust, or reconcile, treat it as planning a corrective social move (choosing what to say or do to undo the harm), not as detecting whether harm occurred.
- If the task is whether a statement is literally true, false, or a lie, including a kind white lie told to spare feelings, evaluate it against reality and the speaker's belief; a tactful lie that comforts the listener is not a faux pas at all.
- If the task is to recover an indirect meaning, hint, request, or sarcasm, infer what the speaker really meant beneath the surface rather than whether the surface remark caused offense.
- If the task is to name which emotion a character feels after the act, such as guilt, shame, or embarrassment, appraise the emotional reaction to the act rather than re-deciding whether a misstep occurred.
- If rank, hierarchy, or deference is the dominant issue and any embarrassment is only secondary, treat it as status-and-deference communication instead.
