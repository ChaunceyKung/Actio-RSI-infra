# Power Gap, Escalation, and Hierarchical Communication

## Use When
- Use when a status or authority difference is what makes one communication move right and another wrong (speaking up to a senior, correcting a junior, reporting upward).
- Use when the question is whether to defer, soften, push back, challenge, concede, escalate, or go over the immediate listener's head.
- Use when the same words would be appropriate between equals but become risky or insubordinate because one party outranks the other.
- Use when the best answer is the safest yet most effective move given who holds power and which direction the message travels (up, down, or across the chain).

## Do Not Use When
- Do not use when the task is only to label a role or status relation and no communication action has to be chosen.
- Do not use when both parties hold equal standing and the work is to trade offers or find a compromise between peers.
- Do not use when the real goal is to curate one's own image or first impression to an audience, and rank is not what decides the right move.
- Do not use when the goal is simply to find the most convincing argument that changes the listener's belief, regardless of who outranks whom.

## Decision Variable
which communication action is most appropriate given the direction and size of the power gap, the stakes, and the norms of the setting

## Direct Route Rule
Route here when a status or authority difference is the variable that decides which communication move (defer, push back, challenge, concede, escalate, or report over someone's head) is correct.

## Trigger Checklist
- One party clearly outranks the other, or sits above or below in a chain of command.
- The choice is not what to say but how to deliver it given that rank: defer, hedge, push back, challenge, concede, or escalate.
- A blunt or direct reply might be rude, unsafe, or career-damaging because of the status gap.
- The immediate listener may be unable or unwilling to act, raising whether to go over their head.
- The right move depends on the stakes and the institution's norms for speaking up or reporting.

## Workflow
- Map the power structure: who outranks whom, and which direction the message must travel (up to a senior, down to a junior, or across to a peer).
- Name the communication goal: inform, request, object, correct, escalate, concede, or defer.
- Read the stakes and the norms: how serious is the issue, and can one safely push back or escalate here, or do the setting and rank demand deference first.
- Choose the move calibrated to that gap and its risks: defer or soften when rank is steep and stakes are low; push back politely or challenge when the issue is serious and the channel is safe; escalate or go over the listener's head only when the immediate listener cannot or will not act and the stakes justify it.
- Commit to the concrete action: the wording, the channel (private vs public, in-person vs written), the timing, or the escalation step, not just the abstract social label.

## Special Case
**Escalate or go over a head only when the direct move cannot work**: When the immediate listener has dismissed the issue or lacks the authority to fix it, and the stakes are high enough, the calibrated move shifts from arguing in place to escalating through a more formal or higher channel, chosen to fit rather than openly defy the hierarchy.

Scene: A junior engineer's proposal is rejected by his direct leader, who doubts it can work technically and financially. The engineer still believes it is sound and wants to change the leader's mind.
Question: What is the most appropriate move across this power gap?
Answer logic: Pleading personal effort is too weak upward, and quietly lobbying other colleagues to pressure the leader goes around the chain and reads as insubordinate. The calibrated upward move is to bring objective external evidence the leader can weigh, such as documented successful cases of similar plans elsewhere, which respects the leader's authority while still making the case.

## Boundary Exit Rule
- If both parties hold equal standing and the work is to trade concessions and reach a deal, treat it as peer bargaining: look for the exchange that satisfies both sides, not a rank-calibrated move.
- If the real goal is to manage one's own image, likeability, or first impression to an audience, solve it as self-presentation: shape how one comes across, since rank is not the variable deciding the move here.
- If the goal is purely to find the argument that shifts the listener's belief or desire, regardless of who outranks whom, solve it as persuasion: pick the most convincing reason, not the move dictated by status.
- If the task is only to infer feelings, beliefs, or hidden intentions and no communication action has to be chosen, solve it as a mental-state inference about that person, not as a hierarchy decision.
- If the move is an apology repairing an offense already committed, treat it as repair: restore the relationship after the fact, rather than choosing a power-aware first move.
