# Hidden Emotion and Atypical Emotion

## Use When
- Use when the scene asks how a character truly feels rather than what they displayed or said.
- Use when a character's outward expression, behavior, or stated words conflict with their likely inner feeling.
- Use when the key task is to infer suppressed, masked, reversed, or unusually appraised emotion.
- Use when an outcome looks ordinary by default but the character reacts atypically because it is judged against their own specific desire, goal, or expectation.
- Use when the character plausibly feels two opposite emotions at once and the question asks about that mixed state.

## Do Not Use When
- Do not use when the emotion stems from responsibility, blame, guilt, shame, pride, or gratitude tied to who caused harm or deserves credit; that is moral-emotion reasoning about norms and accountability, a different scene.
- Do not use when the scene is mainly about what someone saw, knew, or believed instead of what they felt.
- Do not use when the task is to decode an indirect utterance, hint, or sarcasm and the emotion itself is not what the question targets.
- Do not use when the outcome simply meets the character's ordinary expectation with no conflicting desire and no motive to conceal; the plain typical emotion needs no hidden-emotion analysis.

## Decision Variable
why a character's true feeling differs from outward display or ordinary expectation

## Direct Route Rule
Route immediately when the true feeling must be inferred through concealment or a display rule, or through an appraisal made relative to the character's own stated goal or desire rather than the reader's default reaction.

## Trigger Checklist
- The question asks what the person really feels, not what they did or said literally.
- The character has a stated goal, wish, or expectation, and the outcome must be judged against it rather than against the reader's default reaction.
- The person shows one emotion outwardly but the context (a motive to mask, a later private reaction) suggests a different inner state.
- The emotion may be hidden, restrained, inverted, mixed, or atypical given the character's own desire.
- The answer depends on appraising the situation relative to the character's goal or on a display-versus-felt mismatch, not on factual memory, belief tracking, or who is to blame.

## Workflow
- First find the character's OWN stated goal, desire, or expectation in the text (what they hoped for, wanted, or were waiting for). This, not the reader's default reaction, is the yardstick.
- Appraise the actual event RELATIVE TO that personal yardstick: did the outcome meet, exceed, or fall short of what this specific character wanted? An outcome that looks fine by ordinary standards can still be a letdown if it misses the character's particular wish (wanted a computer, got a bicycle = disappointed).
- From that goal-relative appraisal, name the FELT emotion. If no personal goal is stated and nothing conflicts with the ordinary appraisal, the typical expected emotion is the right answer; do not force an 'atypical' label without a conflicting desire.
- Now decide what the question is asking. If it asks what the character FEELS or REALLY feels, answer with the felt emotion from the steps above.
- Only if the question contrasts shown versus hidden emotion (how they appear vs. how they truly feel, or why they hide it) do a separate display-rule check: is there a motive to mask, fake, or invert the expression (politeness, wanting permission, protecting someone, saving face)? The visible display can then differ from the felt emotion, and you report whichever the question targets.
- Watch for genuinely mixed feelings: a character can feel two opposite emotions at once (glad a friend won, yet sad to lose). Keep both rather than collapsing to one.
- If the emotion actually arises from a norm or responsibility appraisal after harm (guilt, shame, pride, gratitude tied to who is to blame or who deserves credit), this is a different scene about moral emotion and accountability; solve it by reasoning about the norm and responsibility, not with the true-feeling-versus-display procedure here.

## Special Case
**Appraise the outcome against the character's own desire, then check for a display rule**: Two separable steps. (1) To get the FELT emotion, measure the event against what THIS character wanted, not against your default reaction. (2) To answer a shown-versus-hidden question, check whether the character has a reason to mask, so the visible display can differ from the felt emotion.

Scene: It is a child's birthday. He has been hoping for a computer or a new game, and instead receives a bicycle. (Variant: a friend hides her stomachache and acts cheerful so her mother will let her go to a party.)
Question: What does the child really feel about the gift? (Variant: how does the friend really feel, and how does she appear?)
Answer logic: Gift case: a bicycle is fine by the reader's default, but appraised against the child's stated wish for a computer or game it falls short, so the felt emotion is disappointment, not happiness. Concealment variant: the felt emotion is sadness (she is unwell and may miss out), while her shown display is cheerful because she has a motive to mask, getting permission to go; report whichever the question asks. Both reduce to: anchor on the character's own goal for the true feeling, and treat display as a separate layer driven by a motive to conceal.

## Boundary Exit Rule
- If the emotion is really about guilt, shame, pride, gratitude, or blame after someone is harmed or helped, the feeling flows from a norm and responsibility appraisal, not from a display-versus-felt mismatch; solve it by asking who is accountable and which norm was broken or upheld, not with the procedure here.
- If the core challenge is what a character knows, saw, or falsely believes rather than what they feel, this is a knowledge or belief problem; track each character's information access and beliefs instead of inferring a concealed emotion.
- If the puzzle is decoding an indirect utterance, hint, sarcasm, or a nonverbal signal whose meaning (not whose emotion) is in question, treat it as interpreting conversational intention, not as recovering a hidden feeling.
- If the outcome simply meets the character's ordinary expectation with no stated conflicting desire and no motive to mask, do not invent an atypical or hidden emotion; the plain, typical reaction is the answer.

## Quick Route Signal
Either a mismatch between what a character shows and what they truly feel (concealed, faked, or reversed), or a reaction that is surprising only because it is judged against the character's own stated goal or desire instead of the observer's default.
