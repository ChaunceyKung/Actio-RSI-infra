# Lies, Half-Truths, and Misdirection

## Use When
- Use when the scene asks whether a speaker lied, told a half-truth, omitted crucial facts, or used wording to create a false impression.
- Use when the key question is whether the utterance is literally true, literally false, or technically true but deceptive.
- Use when the question asks WHY a false or slanted statement was made and the answer is a lie motive: a white lie to protect another's feelings, or a self-serving lie to avoid blame or gain advantage.
- Use when a statement is literally false but you must decide whether the speaker lied on purpose or simply held a sincere but mistaken belief (an involuntary, non-lie error).
- Use when the listener's mistaken belief is caused by what was said, what was left out, or how the message was framed, and you must judge that misleading effect.

## Do Not Use When
- Do not use when the utterance is non-literal but the real question is its INTENDED meaning rather than its truth value, such as irony, sarcasm, or a joke where a false-sounding line is meant to mock or amuse, not to deceive.
- Do not use when the main issue is decoding an honest indirect message, such as a hint, request, warning, complaint, refusal, or invitation, where the speaker is not trying to make the listener believe something false.
- Do not use when the main issue is audience adaptation or persuasion: tailoring a sincerely believed message to a listener's knowledge, motive, or resistance, with no truth-status problem.
- Do not use when the main issue is whether lying is blameworthy, or about guilt, shame, apology, or relationship repair, rather than classifying the utterance's truth value, motive, and misleading effect.

## Decision Variable
how an utterance operates across truth value, motive, and misleading effect

## Direct Route Rule
Route immediately when the central question is the truth status of an utterance, the motive behind a false or slanted statement, or whether wording under partial disclosure misleads the listener.

## Expand With Micro Units

## Trigger Checklist
- The scene asks whether the statement is true, false, or only partially true (often phrased 'Is what X says true?' or 'Does X tell the truth?').
- The scene asks WHY the speaker said something false or slanted, and the candidate answers include lie motives (protect feelings, avoid blame, cover up) versus a sincere mistake.
- The speaker may know more than they say and may be withholding or reframing key facts.
- The wording is chosen to create a false impression, not merely to be polite, ironic, or indirect.
- A listener is meant to end up with an incorrect or incomplete belief, and the answer turns on both the content and the speaker's intent.

## Workflow
- Step 1 - Compare the statement to reality. State the literal content, then check the actual facts in the scene, and classify the words as TRUE, FALSE, or LITERALLY TRUE BUT MISLEADING (correct on a narrow point yet framed to push a wrong conclusion).
- Step 2 - Read the speaker's motive and knowledge at the moment of speaking. If the words are false, ask whether the speaker KNEW they were false. If they sincerely but mistakenly believed the statement (e.g., forgot or never noticed the relevant fact), it is an involuntary error, NOT a real lie. If they knowingly said something false, identify the motive: protecting another person's feelings (white lie), or self-interest such as avoiding blame, escaping trouble, or gaining advantage (egocentric / self-serving lie).
- Step 3 - Judge the misleading effect on the listener. Decide what false or incomplete belief the listener is led to, and whether that effect comes from a direct falsehood, a strategic omission, or a true-but-slanted framing.
- Step 4 - Answer the asked question. For 'is it true' decide true vs. false; for 'why was it said' choose the matching motive (white lie, self-serving lie, or sincere mistake); for 'how does it work' name the mechanism (direct lie, half-truth/omission, cover story, bluff, or technically-true misdirection).
- If the line is non-literal but the point is its intended meaning (irony, sarcasm, joke, or an honest hint) rather than deceiving the listener, this is the wrong scene; solve it as decoding intended meaning, not as truth-status or deception.

## Special Case
**Technically true but misleading**: If the words are factually correct but are chosen to push the listener toward a wrong conclusion, classify the scene as deceptive misdirection (a half-truth), not as an honest indirect message and not as a clean lie.

Scene: A manager asks whether a report is finished. The employee says, 'It is on my desk,' but has not completed it and knows the manager will take that to mean it is done.
Question: Was the employee being truthful?
Answer logic: Step 1 - compare to reality: the words are literally true about the report's location, so this is not a flat lie. Step 2 - motive and knowledge: the employee knows the report is unfinished and that the manager will infer completion, so the framing is chosen on purpose. Step 3 - misleading effect: the manager is led to a false belief about completion status. The line is therefore technically true but misleading - a deceptive half-truth - not innocent indirect speech and not a literal falsehood.

## Boundary Exit Rule
- If the line is non-literal but means the OPPOSITE of its words to mock, criticize, or amuse (irony, sarcasm, or a joke), and the speaker is not trying to make the listener believe a falsehood, treat it as decoding intended meaning, not as a lie. A false-sounding sarcastic line is not deception.
- If the utterance is an honest indirect message - a hint, request, warning, complaint, refusal, reminder, or invitation - where the speaker means something real and is not creating a false belief, solve it as inferring the indirect meaning, not as truth-status play.
- If a statement is literally false but the speaker sincerely believed it (forgot or never knew the true fact), the answer is a sincere mistake; do not classify it as a lie just because the words are false.
- If the scene is really about whether tailoring a sincerely-believed message to a listener's knowledge or resistance succeeds, that is persuasion or audience design, not deception; there is no truth-status problem to resolve.
- If the scene is mainly about whether the lying was wrong, or about apology, guilt, or repairing the relationship after a deception, that is a moral-judgment or social-repair problem; this macro only classifies the utterance (truth value, motive, misleading effect), it does not assign blame.
- If the main question is only who believes what, with no deception or selective disclosure in the utterance itself, solve it as a belief-tracking problem rather than a truthfulness problem.

## Quick Route Signal
The scene asks whether a speaker told the truth or lied, told a half-truth, gave a cover story, or used wording to create a false impression - and may ask the motive (protect feelings, avoid blame) or whether a false statement was even intentional.
