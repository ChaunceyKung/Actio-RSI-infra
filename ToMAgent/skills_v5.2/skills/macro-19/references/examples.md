# macro-19 Examples and Boundaries

## Decision Variable
how to manage the image one presents to others

## Route Signal
The scene asks how to come across, what to disclose, and in what register, so the actor makes the right impression on an audience in a first meeting or formal setting.

## Hard Boundary
- Arguing the listener into a belief or decision is one-way persuasion, a different scene from curating one's own image
- Choosing a move because of a rank or status difference is power-gap communication, not self-presentation
- Apologizing and making amends after causing harm is relationship repair
- Trading offers and concessions toward a compromise is negotiation

## Shortcut To Avoid
- Do not collapse impression management into persuasion: the task is how the actor comes across, not which reasons or offers would move the other party.
- Do not read a self-presentation choice as deference to power just because the audience is senior; check whether the live decision is the actor's image or a rank-driven move.
- Do not answer with the actor's hidden feelings or with a later apology when the question asks for the fitting outward presentation now.
- Do not give a generic 'be polite' answer that ignores who the audience is and how formal the occasion is.

## Common Failure Modes
- Ignoring the formality of the occasion or who the audience is, and giving a one-size-fits-all polite answer
- Over-disclosing or boasting, dumping the whole life story or self-praise instead of leading with what is relevant to this audience
- Confusing self-presentation with persuading, and answering as if the goal were to argue the listener into a belief or action
- Confusing self-presentation with deferring to power, and answering as if rank rather than image were the live decision
- Answering with the actor's private attitude instead of the outward conduct the scene asks for
- Misjudging the register, being too casual for a formal occasion or too stiff for a warm one

## Minimal Pair
- Case: A guest meets a respected teacher for the first time and must choose how to introduce themselves to come across as respectful and capable. | Why: The decisive variable is curating one's own first impression for a specific audience and occasion, which is exactly this scene type.
- Case: A worker wants a colleague to swap desks and must choose what to offer the colleague to win agreement. | Why: Here the goal is to move the listener's decision with an offer, which is one-way persuasion about the other party, not curating one's own image, so it belongs to a different scene.

## Boundary Stress Test
- A formal greeting is requested, but the real decision is which move to make across a status gap, such as whether to challenge a senior.
- A polished message is requested, but the real task is conceding a point to reach a compromise in a negotiation.
- A first meeting is described, but the real goal is to win the other party over with reasons or offers, which is persuasion.
- A poised first impression is described, but the real goal is to make amends for a prior offense.
- The actor wants to look confident, but the question is actually asking what they privately feel underneath.

## Generalization Note
This scene type covers reusable impression-management situations across introductions, etiquette, formal messages, interviews, and other self-presentation choices, so a matching scene routes here directly: the unifying question is always how to curate the image the actor shows to a given audience, not how to move that audience or where the actor stands in a hierarchy.
