# Harm and Responsibility

## Use When
- Use when the question asks whether a person deserves blame for a harm, loss, injury, or damage.
- Use when the answer turns on intent or negligence: was the harm deliberate, reckless, careless, or a pure accident.
- Use when the scene asks who is responsible for a harmful outcome and whether holding them at fault is justified.
- Use when a moral emotion (guilt, remorse) is the cue but the underlying judgment is whether the actor is at fault for the harm.

## Do Not Use When
- Do not use when the question only asks WHAT produced an outcome with no judgment of fault; naming a cause without weighing blame is causal attribution.
- Do not use when the issue is whether a remark was rude, awkward, or embarrassing regardless of any harm; that is faux pas and social tact.
- Do not use when the task is what to DO after the harm to mend the relationship, such as apologizing; that is relationship repair.
- Do not use when the question only names which emotion the actor feels and blame is not the contested point; that is reading moral emotions.

## Decision Variable
blameworthiness for harm (does this person deserve fault, given cause plus intent or negligence)

## Trigger Checklist
- Is there an actual harm: damage, injury, loss, or wrongful consequence to someone?
- Does the question judge fault or blame, not merely ask what caused the outcome?
- Is intent or negligence in play: deliberate, reckless, careless, or unavoidable accident?
- Would the verdict change if the person had known or could have prevented it?
- Is the contested point fault for harm, rather than rudeness, fairness, or how to apologize?

## Workflow
- Confirm a real harm occurred (damage, loss, injury). If none, this is not a harm-responsibility scene; check for faux pas/tact instead.
- Check what the question demands. If it only asks WHY the outcome happened or WHAT caused it, stop and route to causal attribution; cause is not the same as blame.
- Identify the causal actor: who did the act that led to the harm, directly or by failing to act.
- Grade intent or negligence: deliberate harm and reckless or careless harm raise blame; a genuinely unforeseeable accident does not.
- Test foreseeability and control: could this person reasonably have known the risk or prevented it? Knew or should have known raises blame; truly could not raises it little or none.
- Assign the verdict: blame only when cause AND culpability (intent or negligence) both hold; if there is cause but no culpability, the person is the cause yet not blameworthy.

## Special Case
**Cause is not blame: the accidental but blameless actor**: A person can be the direct cause of a harm and still not be blameworthy. Blame requires culpability on top of causation: intent, recklessness, or negligence. If the actor could not reasonably have known or prevented the harm, they caused it but do not deserve blame. The matching moral emotion is guilt or remorse over having caused the loss, which is distinct from being at fault.

Scene: A waitress rushing through a busy cafe accidentally knocks a cup, splashing coffee onto a customer's important documents and ruining them. She did not notice and never intended any harm.
Question: Is the waitress to blame for ruining the customer's documents?
Answer logic: She is the cause of the damage, so she bears responsibility for the outcome. But the harm was an unintended accident with no recklessness or negligence, so she is the cause without culpable fault: low or no blame. Treating her as deliberately at fault overstates it, while denying she bears any responsibility at all (it is not her concern) understates the responsibility she does carry as the cause.

## Boundary Exit Rule
- Exit if the question only asks what produced the outcome, the reason it happened, with no judgment of who is at fault; an accidental cause is still a cause, and naming it is causal attribution.
- Exit if the harm is absent and the issue is only whether a remark or act was rude, awkward, or embarrassing; that is faux pas and social tact.
- Exit if the question names the emotion the actor feels and blame itself is not contested; that is reading moral emotions such as guilt or shame.
- Exit if the task is what the wrongdoer should DO next to restore the relationship, such as apologize or make amends; that is relationship repair.
