# macro-06 Examples and Boundaries

## Decision Variable
what emotion or judgment follows from responsibility, harm, and norm evaluation

## Route Signal
A morally loaded outcome is followed by a question about emotion or judgment, and the answer turns on responsibility, harm, intent, duty, excuse, or repair.

## Hard Boundary
- A plain appraisal of a good or bad outcome with no norm or responsibility (disappointment at an unwanted gift, fear at a noise, joy at good news) is outside this macro; that is read off the gap between wanted and received, with no moral label.
- Merely detecting WHETHER an inappropriate or awkward remark was made (a faux pas), with no question about which emotion follows, is outside this macro; that is offense detection, answered yes or no.
- Nonverbal intention reading is outside this macro unless the cue is evidence of guilt, shame, blame, or remorse.
- Indirect speech interpretation is outside this macro unless the utterance functions as apology, accusation, confession, or moral repair.
- Lie detection and misdirection are outside this macro unless the deception is being evaluated as a moral offense.

## Shortcut To Avoid
- Do not infer guilt from a bad outcome alone, and do not withhold guilt just because the act began with a false belief or an accident once the actor learns they caused the harm.
- Do not default to a basic or self-serving emotion label (disappointed, indifferent, surprised, proud, satisfied) when a responsibility, harm, or norm appraisal is clearly the point; the correct answer is the moral emotion.
- Do not treat apology as proof of full responsibility; people may apologize from sympathy, politeness, or repair motives.
- Do not treat lack of visible sadness as lack of guilt; the person may suppress emotion or feel shame privately.
- Do not assign equal blame to all participants when knowledge, power, duty, and control differ, and do not ignore the victim's perspective when the question is about resentment, forgiveness, or fairness.

## Common Failure Modes
- Picking a basic emotion label (disappointed, indifferent, surprised, happy) or a self-serving one (proud, satisfied) when the scene clearly calls for a moral emotion such as guilt, shame, or indignation.
- Misattributing whose responsibility it is, for example assigning guilt to a bystander, blaming the victim, or feeling for the wrong character because the perspective in the question was not tracked.
- Confusing guilt with shame: choosing shame (damaged self-image, public exposure) when the scene stresses harm done and personal responsibility, or choosing guilt when the scene is really about loss of face before others.
- Confusing regret or sadness with guilt when the actor had no meaningful responsibility, or, conversely, withholding guilt just because the act started from a false belief or an accident even after the actor learns they caused the harm.
- Over-blaming accidental harm while ignoring false belief, lack of knowledge, or lack of control, or under-blaming negligence and deliberate omission because the actor did not intend the harm.
- Treating rule-following as automatically moral, or treating an apology as proof of full responsibility, without checking actual harm, fairness, knowledge, and control.

## Minimal Pair
- Case: A person takes money from a box, later realizes the money was not theirs to use, and the question asks how they feel. They feel guilt because they used what was not theirs. | Why: A norm (property and fairness) was violated and the focal person is responsible, so the feeling is a moral emotion. This is in scope: map the responsibility appraisal to guilt rather than to a neutral or self-justifying label.
- Case: A person hoped for a computer but receives a bicycle as a gift, and the question asks how they feel. They feel disappointed. | Why: Same surface form (an event followed by an emotion question) but no norm, harm, or responsibility is involved, only the gap between what was wanted and what was received. This belongs to ordinary outcome-based emotion, NOT here: do not reach for guilt or shame when only plain disappointment is at stake.
- Case: After someone makes a tactless remark to a classmate who just lost a contest, the question asks only whether anyone said something inappropriate. | Why: An offense may be present, but the task is to DETECT whether a faux pas occurred, not to name which moral emotion follows. This belongs to offense detection, NOT here: this macro would take over only if the question then asked what the speaker or the hurt party feels.

## Boundary Stress Test
- A person apologizes after an accident: decide whether the apology signals guilt, politeness, sympathy, or repair.
- A person causes harm while following orders: separate causal responsibility, role duty, power pressure, and moral blame.
- A person intended a harmless prank but caused serious harm: weigh intention against foreseeable risk and negligence.
- A person is publicly criticized for a mistake: distinguish shame from guilt, and distinguish self-image damage from responsibility.
- Several people contributed to the harm: distribute responsibility according to knowledge, control, duty, and causal contribution.

## Generalization Note
This macro generalizes across false-emotion and moral-judgment scenes, real-world apology and blame scenes, and social conflicts where emotion is not just a reaction to outcome but an appraisal of responsibility under norms.
