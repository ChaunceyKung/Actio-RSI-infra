# macro-12 Examples and Boundaries

## Decision Variable
whether anyone in the scene said or did something inappropriate that hurts, embarrasses, or threatens another person's face, especially when the speaker was unaware of a fact that makes the remark hurtful

## Route Signal
The scene asks whether anyone said or did something inappropriate, hurtful, embarrassing, or offensive, often featuring a speaker who unknowingly says something that wounds a listener because the speaker lacked a fact the listener holds.

## Hard Boundary
- An offense is already identified and the task is repair, apology, or reconciliation.
- The task is truth evaluation or lie detection, including kind white lies.
- The task is recovering an indirect or implied meaning, hint, or sarcasm.
- The task is naming the emotion (guilt, shame, embarrassment) that follows an act.
- The task is rank and deference, action selection, preference conflict, or perspective taking.

## Shortcut To Avoid
- Do not call a remark a faux pas just because the topic is sensitive or a rivalry exists; check whether anyone is actually hurt and whether the speaker was ignorant of the fact that makes it hurtful.
- Do not assume that because the speaker meant no harm there was no faux pas; lack of intent is exactly what defines the classic faux pas.
- Do not flip the other way and call deliberate, fully-informed rudeness a 'faux pas'; a faux pas requires the speaker to have been unaware.
- Do not default to 'yes, something inappropriate was said'; many control stories contain only neutral or kind remarks, and the correct answer there is 'no faux pas'.
- Do not score the remark for truth or apology when the question only asks whether it was inappropriate.

## Common Failure Modes
- Over-detecting: labeling a neutral or kind remark a faux pas because the situation feels tense or a sensitive topic appears.
- Missing the speaker-ignorance condition: judging only that a remark sounds blunt without checking whether the speaker knew the fact that makes it hurtful.
- Confusing deliberate rudeness with a faux pas: treating a remark the speaker knowingly and intentionally made as the classic unaware blunder.
- Hallucinating offense in a clean control story where everyone is polite, instead of answering 'no one said anything inappropriate'.
- Drifting into repair, truth-scoring, or emotion-naming instead of simply deciding whether a misstep occurred.

## Minimal Pair
- Case: A contest winner casually tells an absent rival that his entry was 'a bit of a shame,' not knowing how much the rival wanted to win. | Why: A hurtful remark made in ignorance of the fact that makes it hurtful: the classic faux pas, decided by the speaker-ignorance test, and squarely in scope.
- Case: A nephew privately dislikes his aunt's new hat but tells her it looks lovely when she asks. | Why: A kind, tactful white lie that hurts no one; nothing inappropriate is said. The only live question is whether the statement is literally true, which is a truthfulness matter and belongs to deception evaluation, not faux-pas detection.

## Boundary Stress Test
- If the speaker had known the listener's hidden fact, would they still have made the remark? If yes, it is deliberate offense, not a classic faux pas; if no, it is a faux pas.
- If every line in the story is neutral or kind, is the correct answer 'no one said anything inappropriate' rather than a forced 'yes'?
- If the remark is a comforting untruth, is the task really about whether it was true rather than whether it was offensive?
- If the question asks how to make amends, has the task shifted from detecting the offense to repairing it?

## Generalization Note
This macro covers any scene whose question is whether a remark or act was inappropriate, offensive, awkward, or embarrassing, across compositions, jokes, disclosures, and chance comments. Its sharpest case is the classic faux pas, where a speaker unknowingly wounds a listener; its boundaries are the polite control story (answer: no faux pas), the kind white lie (a truth question), and deliberate rudeness (intentional offense, not an unaware blunder).
