# Moral Emotion and Responsibility Judgment

## Use When
- Use when the question asks what someone feels or how someone is judged after causing, allowing, noticing, or being associated with harm or a norm violation.
- Use when guilt, shame, blame, embarrassment from wrongdoing, apology pressure, forgiveness, or responsibility is the main driver of the answer.
- Use when the key distinction is whether the actor intended the harm, knew the risk, could have prevented it, or violated a social or moral rule.

## Do Not Use When
- Do not use when the emotion is a plain appraisal of a good or bad outcome with no norm, harm, or responsibility (for example, disappointment at getting an unwanted gift, fear at a strange noise, joy at good news). That is ordinary outcome-based emotion: read it off the gap between what the person wanted and what they got, without any blame or moral judgment.
- Do not use when the only task is to DETECT whether someone said or did something inappropriate or awkward (a faux pas), with no question about which emotion follows. That is offense detection: check whether a speaker said something hurtful or unwarranted given what they did and did not know, and answer yes or no, rather than mapping to a moral emotion.
- Do not use when the main task is decoding a wink, smile, glance, or gesture to read intention; that requires interpreting the nonverbal cue itself, not judging responsibility, unless the cue is offered as evidence of guilt, shame, or remorse.
- Do not use when the main task is figuring out what an indirect or non-literal utterance means; that requires recovering the speaker's intended meaning, not assigning blame.
- Do not use when the central question is whether a statement is true, misleading, or deceptive; that requires comparing the statement to the facts and to the speaker's knowledge, unless the deception itself is being morally evaluated as an offense.

## Decision Variable
what emotion or judgment follows from responsibility, harm, and norm evaluation

## Direct Route Rule
Route immediately when guilt, shame, blame, or responsibility drives the emotion.

## Expand With Micro Units
- Moral Emotion: the responsibility or norm appraisal behind the emotion (micro-30)
- Emotion Regulation: the character's internal emotion-management strategy (micro-33)
- Harm and Responsibility: blameworthiness for harm (does this person deserve fault, given cause plus intent or negligence) (micro-43)

## Trigger Checklist
- A person caused, failed to prevent, benefited from, or is accused of a bad outcome.
- The scene contains harm, unfairness, betrayal, rule-breaking, negligence, or social/moral disapproval.
- The question asks who feels guilty, ashamed, blamed, responsible, forgiven, angry, resentful, or morally judged.
- The answer depends on intention, knowledge, avoidability, duty, apology, compensation, or excuse.
- A character's self-evaluation or others' evaluation matters more than the raw event outcome.

## Workflow
- Fix the perspective first: whose emotion or judgment does the question ask about? The answer almost always hinges on whether that specific person is the one who caused harm, the one harmed, or an observer. Track that role precisely; misattributing whose responsibility it is is the most common way to get this wrong.
- Name the morally relevant event: harm, offense, broken promise, unfairness, betrayal, negligence, or violation of a norm or duty. If no norm was met or broken and no one was harmed or helped on purpose, this is not a moral-emotion scene.
- Separate outcome from responsibility: a bad result alone does not imply guilt or blame. Ask who actually caused the harm or met the standard, and by what action or omission.
- Check the causal role of the focal person: did they directly cause it, enable it, fail in a duty to warn or protect, merely witness it, or get wrongly associated with it? Even a false belief at the time does not erase guilt once the person learns they caused harm or used what was not theirs.
- Check mental state and control: did they intend the harm, knowingly risk it, act carelessly, act by accident, or act under a false belief, and could they reasonably have acted otherwise?
- Map the appraisal to the specific moral emotion. GUILT: I caused harm or violated a norm or duty (including by omission), and I hold myself responsible. SHAME: I am exposed before others as deficient or in the wrong, so the focus is my damaged standing rather than the harm itself. PRIDE: I met or exceeded a valued standard through my own effort or virtue. GRATITUDE: I benefited from another person's intentional kindness or sacrifice. INDIGNATION or righteous ANGER: another person violated a norm against me or someone else, so blame points outward. RESENTMENT: I was treated unfairly. Pick the moral label, not a basic stand-in: do not answer plain disappointment, indifference, or self-serving satisfaction when a responsibility appraisal is clearly in play.
- Distinguish guilt from shame when both seem possible: if the scene stresses the harm done and the actor's responsibility for it, choose guilt; if it stresses public exposure, loss of face, or a damaged self-image, choose shame.
- Adjust for repair and aggravation: apology, confession, compensation, and sincere remorse reduce blame and support forgiveness; denial, concealment, deliberate withholding, or repeated harm increases blame. Do not treat an apology as proof of full responsibility.
- Answer from the perspective fixed in step 1: the wrongdoer's likely feeling, the victim's judgment, the observer's blame, or the community's norm evaluation.

## Special Case
**Accidental harm versus culpable harm**: If the harm was accidental and the actor lacked knowledge, intent, and reasonable control, reduce blame; the actor may still feel regret or sadness, but guilt and external blame are weaker unless they ignored a duty or risk.

Scene: Maya brings cookies to a meeting, not knowing that one contains nuts because the box was mislabeled. Leo, who is allergic, eats one and becomes sick. Maya is upset and apologizes.
Question: Should Maya feel guilty or be blamed?
Answer logic: The harm is real, but Maya did not know about the nuts and did not control the mislabeling. She may feel sorry, regretful, and concerned, but strong blame or guilt is not warranted unless she had a duty to check and failed to do so.

## Boundary Exit Rule
- If the emotion is just a reaction to a good or bad outcome with no norm met or broken and no one responsible (for example, disappointment at an unwanted gift or fear at a strange noise), solve it as an ordinary outcome appraisal: compare what the person wanted to what happened, and read off the resulting feeling, with no blame or moral label.
- If the question only asks WHETHER someone said or did something inappropriate or awkward, and not which emotion follows, solve it as offense detection: judge whether a remark was hurtful or unwarranted given what the speaker did and did not know, and answer yes or no, rather than mapping to a moral emotion.
- If the evidence is primarily a nonverbal cue (a wink, smile, glance, or gesture) and the question asks what intention it conveys, interpret the cue itself rather than judging responsibility, unless the cue is being read as a sign of guilt, shame, or remorse.
- If the question is mainly what an indirect complaint, warning, request, or refusal actually means, recover the speaker's intended meaning rather than assigning blame.
- If the central issue is whether a statement is true, an omission, deceptive, or misleadingly worded, compare the statement against the facts and the speaker's knowledge, unless the deception itself is being morally judged as an offense.
