# macro-10 Examples and Boundaries

## Decision Variable
which action, option, or allocation should be chosen when preferences conflict (or which competing desire wins inside one agent)

## Route Signal
Competing wants, between two parties or inside one agent, where the question is what gets chosen, who gets the resource, or which action follows.

## Hard Boundary
- The task is to find the best way to convince or move another person while one party keeps its own goal fixed (one-way persuasion).
- Both sides trade concessions to co-build a joint plan and the question is about that exchange, not about one resulting choice.
- Truthfulness, deception, or indirect meaning of an utterance is central.
- A prior commitment, schedule, or invitation fixes the next action with no real clash of wants.
- Social embarrassment, offense, or apology is the main issue.

## Shortcut To Avoid
- Do not report the preference of the most vividly described character; answer with the party the scene's deciding rule actually favors.
- Do not assume the host or inviter gets their own taste; courtesy usually tips the outcome toward the guest or recipient.
- Do not default to a 50/50 split or compromise unless the scene signals fairness, indifference, or an explicit agreement to share.
- Do not treat a spoken denial as the action; when a desire and an action contradict, predict the action that follows the dominant desire.
- Do not turn the scene into hidden-belief or persuasion analysis when the answer is just the preference-based outcome.

## Common Failure Modes
- Tracking the wrong party's desire, e.g. answering with the host's favorite when the question is about what the guest will get.
- Ignoring a stated priority or a clearly stronger preference and treating both wants as equal.
- Defaulting to a compromise or even split the scene gives no support for.
- On a desire-action contradiction, reporting the stated denial as the true action, or attaching a generic reason the story never gives instead of the scene's specific overriding motive.
- Confusing a pure commitment-follow-through scene with a genuine preference conflict.

## Minimal Pair
- Case: An outdoorsy host who loves hiking invites a quiet, indoor-loving friend to spend the weekend together; the question asks where they will go. | Why: Two opposed preferences and the question asks which one becomes the outcome. The standing rule applies, so the host accommodates the guest and the outcome follows the guest's quiet-indoor preference, the in-scope way this macro resolves a conflict.
- Case: An outdoorsy person wants the hiking trip and the indoor-loving friend resists, and the person works out the most convincing thing to SAY to bring the friend around, while still aiming for the hike. | Why: Near miss: the goal is fixed and the task is the best persuasive move, so it is one-way persuasion (reason about what shifts the listener), not a question of which preference is selected as the outcome.

## Boundary Stress Test
- If both sides keep offering and giving things up to reach an agreed plan, ask whether the question wants that exchange (a negotiation) or one final chosen outcome (this macro).
- If a character is figuring out what to SAY to win someone over while keeping their own goal, that is persuasion, not a preference-conflict outcome.
- If an agent says it does not want something it clearly wants, check that you are predicting the action from the dominant desire and citing the scene's specific reason, not the literal denial.
- If a prior promise or schedule fixes the next move with no real clash of wants, the driver is commitment, not preference conflict.

## Generalization Note
Use this macro whenever a scene resolves to a single choice by mapping competing preferences onto an outcome, whether the conflict is between people or among one agent's own wants, and whatever the setting (a meal, trip, game, task, gift, or shared resource).
